Schellenberg put the argument in a tight form. If a perfectly loving God exists, every person capable of a relationship with God and not resisting one would believe that God exists, because belief is the minimum needed for the relationship to begin. But there are such people, non-resistant non-believers, who have looked and not found. So a perfectly loving God does not exist.

The force of the argument comes from the comparison with human love. A parent who could easily make their existence known to a child who was looking for them, and chose not to, would not be called loving. Theists have to explain why the analogy fails.

They have tried several ways. Pascal thought God shows enough for those who seek and hides enough from those who do not. Others argue that unmistakable evidence would crowd out genuine freedom, or that the "non-resistant" seeker is rarer than it looks, since resistance can be hidden even from oneself. Rea suggests that God's personality may simply not be what we assume, and that silence is not the same as absence.

What an outsider should take from it: the argument puts a real cost on theism, and the replies ask you to accept that God's love works differently from ours in exactly the place where we would most expect it to be the same. For many people this is the argument that moves them more than suffering, because it is about their own search.
