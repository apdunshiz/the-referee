Human minds come fitted with a hair-trigger for detecting agents, a habit of reading purpose into events, and a readiness to accept what trusted elders say. These traits are useful: it is cheaper to mistake a branch for a snake than the reverse. They also generate spirits, ancestors and gods in every society ever studied. Add the social facts, that religions bind groups, enforce cooperation and spread with conquest and trade, and you have an account of religion that needs no gods in it.

The argument against God is that if this account is complete, religious belief is explained as an output of the machinery, and outputs of machinery that fires regardless of whether gods exist cannot be evidence that they do.

The theist's answer, pressed by Barrett among others, is that this is a genetic fallacy: the origin of a belief says nothing about its truth. Our belief in other people's minds also comes from fast, automatic systems, and no one calls that belief unjustified. A God who wanted to be findable might have built the disposition in on purpose.

The reply is about calibration. Belief in other minds is checked a thousand times a day; belief in gods is not, and the same machinery that produces one God produces Zeus, ancestor spirits and the evil eye.

What an outsider should take from it: this does not show God does not exist. It removes one reason people have had for thinking he does, namely that belief in him is universal, and it puts the burden on the arguments rather than on the feeling.
