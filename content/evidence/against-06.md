The largest controlled trial of intercessory prayer, with 1,800 cardiac patients, found no benefit; patients who knew they were being prayed for did slightly worse. Decades of investigation into reported miracles, at Lourdes and elsewhere, have produced cures that are rare and unexplained but never one that is clearly impossible for a natural process. Lourdes's medical committee lists 72 cures as unexplained by current science; the latest, a recovery from ALS, is in a disease where natural reversals are also documented.

The argument from this is straightforward. If God acts in the world in response to prayer, as most believers hold, that action should be visible in aggregate. It is not. So either God does not act, or God does not exist.

Theists reply that the studies test a caricature: a vending-machine God who answers anonymous requests on schedule. Most theologies say God answers prayer in ways that fit his purposes, not the experimenter's. Keener catalogues hundreds of contemporary miracle reports, many with medical records, and argues that the blanket dismissal of them is a prior commitment, not a finding.

Skeptics answer that an effect which only appears when it cannot be checked is not an effect, and that most reported miracles shrink under investigation, which lowers the weight of the rest. Hume went further and said no testimony could ever establish a miracle; many philosophers, including the non-theist John Earman, now judge that claim too strong.

What an outsider should take from it: the evidence weighs against the kind of God who intervenes visibly and often. It leaves untouched the kind who does not, which is the kind most philosophical theists now defend; skeptics read that as a retreat, theists as a correction of a crude picture.
