Phineas Gage took an iron rod through his frontal lobe in 1848 and his friends said he was no longer Gage, though historians have since found that he recovered enough to work for several years as a stagecoach driver in Chile. Since then neuroscience has filled in the picture: a lesion here removes the ability to recognise faces, a tumour there changes a mild man's character, a drug lifts depression, dementia erases the self a little at a time. Everything we call the mind tracks the brain.

The argument against God comes in two steps. First, this is what we would expect if the mind is what the brain does, and not what we would expect if the mind is an immaterial soul merely using the brain. Second, most religions need the person to survive death, and the soul was the standard way of getting that. If there is no soul, the afterlife needs a miracle, and the religions that promised it on the strength of the soul lose a foundation.

Theists have two responses. Dualists like Swinburne say that correlation is not identity and that a soul could depend on the brain for its operation in this life without being the brain. A number of Christian philosophers, such as Peter van Inwagen and Nancey Murphy, simply concede the point: human beings are physical, and survival is a matter of God raising the body, which New Testament scholars such as Joel Green argue is what the earliest texts taught.

What an outsider should take from it: this is strong evidence against the soul as traditionally pictured and against the easy afterlife that came with it. It is weaker against God as such, since a God who can create can also recreate, and several theologies never needed a ghost in the machine.
