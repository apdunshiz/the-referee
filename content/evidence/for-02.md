The cosmological constant, the strength of the strong nuclear force, the mass ratio of protons and neutrons, the initial smoothness of the early universe: change any of these by a small amount and you get a universe with no stars, no chemistry heavier than hydrogen, or no stable matter at all. This is not an apologist's claim. It is in the textbooks and is defended by physicists who have no religious interest in it, several of whom prefer a multiverse precisely because they find the tuning real and in need of explanation.

The live question is what to make of it. Four answers are on the table. A designer chose the values. There are very many universes with different values and we are, unsurprisingly, in a habitable one. Or a deeper theory will one day show the values could not have been otherwise. Or the values are simply brute facts, the most common answer among the researchers and philosophers surveyed. Each answer has costs: a designer is a large and unexplained addition; a multiverse is unobserved and the mechanisms that generate it tend to need tuning themselves; a deeper theory is a promissory note; a brute fact gives up on explanation.

The sharpest objection is technical. To say a range is improbable you need a probability distribution over the possible values, and nobody has one for the constants of nature. Defenders reply that physicists already reason this way when they call the cosmological constant "unnaturally" small.

What an outsider should take from it: this is, with the moral argument, the strongest piece of evidence on the "for" side, it is taken seriously across the spectrum, and it supports a designer only as one of three live explanations.
