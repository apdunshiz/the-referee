Wigner, a Nobel laureate with no theological agenda, called it unreasonable: mathematical structures invented for their own sake keep turning out to describe physical reality, often decades later and with astonishing precision. Why should a universe be like that? And why should creatures evolved to find food be able to follow the description?

The theistic answer is that both facts are what you would expect if the world and the minds in it come from the same rational source. Nature is intelligible because it was made to be understood.

The non-theistic answers are substantial. Any universe that lasts long enough to produce observers will have regularities, and only such universes get observed. Mathematics is partly shaped by the physical world it was built to describe. And the history of mathematics is full of structures that fit nothing. Some physicists suggest that our sense of the fit is a selection effect: we remember the successes.

What an outsider should take from it: a genuine puzzle, more often felt than argued, with respectable answers on both sides. It adds a little weight to the "for" column for those who already find a rational universe suggestive, and almost none for those who do not.
