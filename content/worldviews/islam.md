Islam is the second-largest religion and the one whose central claim is most like a single, checkable object: a book. Muslims hold that the Quran is not inspired writing about God but God's own words, preserved without alteration. Manuscript evidence supports the preservation better than skeptics once expected. Whether the words are God's is a different question, and the traditional argument for it, that no human could produce such a text, convinces those who already read it as scripture and few others.

Its monotheism is simpler in structure than Christianity's and its practice is more unified than Judaism's; five daily prayers and a shared law give it a coherence of life that outsiders often find impressive. Its intellectual tradition produced serious philosophy and serious science for several centuries.

Its weak points from the outside are the late and legendary character of the biographical sources, the retrospective reading of science into the text, and the places where its law, as traditionally interpreted, conflicts with what most outsiders now take to be basic rights. Muslim scholars contest all of these, and the contest is live.

An outsider notices that Islam's case rests on the Quran in the way Christianity's rests on the resurrection, that the two claims are evaluated by the same standards here, and that each tradition finds the other's central evidence unconvincing for reasons that apply equally to its own.
