# Buddhism: re-examination of the five scores

## Summary

Current total 29/50. Proposed total 27/50 (history 6, consistency 6, explanation 7, testability 3, moral 5).

The research confirms the general shape of the file: a historical founder, a coherent practical core, a strong diagnosis of suffering, and a mixed moral record. It also finds that three lines were scored more generously for Buddhism than the same rule gave other traditions, and one line less generously. Buddhism's devotional strands promise answered prayer as plainly as Hinduism's, so the prayer-trial deduction applies (testability −1). Its monastic code subordinates nuns to monks in every surviving Vinaya, which is an exclusion written into its structure of the kind Islam was docked for (moral −1). Its no-self doctrine strains not only against rebirth but against the compassion it is meant to ground, a tension its own best defenders have not closed (consistency −1). Going the other way, Buddhism has a continuous, documented community from the Ashokan edicts onward and was the only tradition with one not to receive the point (history +1). The meditation-research "held" claim survives, but only in a weak form, and the explanation score survives only because the rule's inherited-cosmology clause overrides the tradition's own refusal to answer cosmological questions; both are flagged. The state-Buddhism coercion cases (Tibet, imperial Japan, Bhutan) fall into classes already deducted and confirm rather than lower the score.

## Historical footing

**Current:** 5, confidence 60. "Start at 5. Foundation that holds: a well-attested founder, a teacher of the fifth century BCE, with core teachings consistent across early sources (plus 1). Gone against: those sources were transmitted orally for centuries before being written, and the biographical material around him is treated as legend by most scholars and many Buddhists (minus 1). Five."

**Evidence:**
- The Buddha's historicity is accepted across the field; his dates are not. The Stanford Encyclopedia gives "fl. circa 450 BCE" and notes that the traditional 560–480 BCE has been revised, with many scholars placing his death around 405 BCE (Siderits, SEP "Buddha", rev. 2023) [1]. Bechert's 1988 Göttingen symposium concluded the 480 date "can no longer be upheld"; no consensus has replaced it [2]. Uncertainty of a century about a founder's dates does not touch his existence, so the +1 stands.
- Gombrich's *What the Buddha Thought* (2009) argues the Pali texts preserve a coherent thinker whose allusions to Brahmanical and Jain ideas fit a single historical context; Gethin's review accepts the core while warning that Gombrich underrates later development [3]. This is the strongest case for the "consistent across early sources" clause.
- Against an "early, stable text": the canon was oral for roughly four centuries, written in Sri Lanka about 29 BCE on the chronicles' testimony, and the earliest surviving manuscripts are the Gandhāran birch-bark scrolls of about the first century CE, which belong to a different school and recension (Salomon 1999) [4][5]. Mahāyāna sūtras composed centuries later are attributed to the Buddha. No +1 for text, and the file is right not to give one.
- Late, legendary biography: the full life stories (Buddhacarita, Lalitavistara, Nidānakathā) are centuries later; the SEP notes the sources "disagree in important respects and may reflect sectarian editing" [1]. The −1 matches the −1 Christianity took for the gospels.
- Continuous, documented community: Ashoka's edicts (c. 250 BCE) address the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha by name and recommend specific texts (Bairat edict) [6]; the Sri Lankan sangha is documented epigraphically from the second century BCE and continuously since; East Asian and Tibetan monastic lineages are documented from the first millennium. Buddhism died out in India by the thirteenth century, but the rule asks for a continuous community, not one in the homeland (Judaism got its +1 despite exile).
- Not scored: Coningham's 2013 Lumbini shrine (nothing in the layer is identifiably Buddhist) [7]; Abhidharma cosmology (Mount Meru), which conflicts with the record but which the worldview does not rest on, and which the Dalai Lama says Buddhism "must abandon" [8]. Hinduism's parallel myths were "not a failed claim."

**Rule applied:** Start at 5. Holds: well-attested founder (+1); continuous, documented community (+1). Gone against: biographical sources written late with legend (−1). No early stable text (0). Six.

**Proposed:** 5→6. Strongest reason: Christianity, Judaism and Hinduism each received +1 for a continuous, documented community; Buddhism's sangha is documented from Ashoka to today and was simply not credited. Confidence 65.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh Buddhism's disappearance from India as breaking continuity, which he did not do for Judaism's exile.

## Internal consistency

**Current:** 7, confidence 60. "Start at 8. One core doctrine needs careful definition: rebirth without a self that is reborn, which its schools address with sophistication but not to everyone's satisfaction. Seven."

**Evidence:**
- The rule names "no-self with rebirth" itself. The SEP states the objection: if nothing transmigrates, "how could the being in the next life be the same person as the being in this life?" and karmic retribution "would be just only if the recipient is the same person as the agent" [1]. The best reply (Siderits) is that persons are conventionally real and causal series carry moral properties without a self. Karma-without-an-agent is the same tension stated from the moral side, not a second doctrine; the SEP "Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy" entry treats them together and notes the mind-stream was introduced precisely "to avoid the implications of a self" [9]. The Pudgalavāda schools posited a person "largely because karma needs a bearer of moral responsibility" [10]: the gap was felt from within.
- No-self with compassion is a separate strain. Śāntideva argues that because there is no self, suffering is ownerless and should be removed wherever it occurs. Paul Williams (*Altruism and Reality*, 1998) argues suffering "makes no sense without a self" and the argument undermines the bodhisattva path; Siderits replied in *Philosophy East and West* 50(3); Stephen Harris (JBE 2011) surveys Williams, Siderits and Pettit and concludes "all of these various reconfigurations of the argument fail to be convincing" [11][12][13]. The SEP Śāntideva entry says the question became central "largely as a result of" Williams's criticism [12]. This is a core doctrine (the ethic's ground) that needs careful definition on the tradition's own best statement, the same kind of item naturalism was docked for ("binding obligation in a world of particles").
- Nirvana as the attainment of no one: the Buddha set aside whether a Tathāgata exists after death; the later fire simile says the question rests on a false presupposition [1][14]. Collins (*Selfless Persons*, 1982) finds the conventional/ultimate reconciliation has "the flavour of rationalisations after the event" [15]. This is the no-self tension at its terminus rather than a new doctrine; counting it separately would be defensible but would double the same difficulty.
- Two truths: a device for dissolving the tensions above, not a doctrine in tension. Garfield and Priest read Nāgārjuna as affirming true contradictions at the limits of thought; Tillemans and others in the 2013 *PEW* special issue argue the two-truths structure keeps the contradictions out [16][17]. One school's reading, contested from within: no deduction on the tradition's best statement.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Core doctrines needing careful definition: no-self with rebirth and karma, one doctrine (−1); no-self as the ground of compassion and obligation (−1). Nirvana-of-no-one folded into the first; two truths a solution, not a tension. Six.

**Proposed:** 7→6. Strongest reason: the Williams–Siderits–Harris literature shows the tradition's best defenders have not agreed how no-self grounds the compassion the ethic rests on, which is the mirror image of the obligation-in-a-world-of-particles deduction naturalism took. Confidence 60.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to treat the compassion argument as part of the one no-self problem already deducted; conversely, if he counts nirvana-of-no-one separately, the score is 5.

## Explanatory reach

**Current:** 7, confidence 55. "Start at 2. Answers all four: a cosmos of cyclic world-systems, inherited and counted like everyone else's; minds, through a phenomenology of attention that psychology has partly confirmed; obligation, through compassion grounded in no-self; suffering, its central diagnosis: plus 4. The diagnosis of suffering is treated across the divide as a distinctive resource: plus 1. Seven."

**Evidence:**
- The unanswered questions (avyākata; MN 63, MN 72) include whether the cosmos is eternal or finite. The Buddha declines them as "distractions insofar as answering them would not lead to the cessation of the defilements" and compares the questioner to a man refusing treatment for an arrow wound [1][14]. This is the clearest case in any tradition of the "honest refusal" the rule says is not an answer. The file's own body text says so: "Buddhism declines to answer and says the questions do not lead to the end of suffering. An outsider can admire the discipline of that and still count it as silence."
- Against that, the rule also says "an inherited cosmology counts as an answer for everyone who has one," and Buddhism has one: cyclic world-systems that expand and contract, with beings reborn from the Ābhassara realm (Aggañña Sutta, DN 27; Abhidharmakośa ch. 3) [19]. Gombrich reads the Aggañña as satire of Brahmanical cosmogony that the tradition later took literally [3][19]; Buddhaghosa and Vasubandhu did take it as cosmology. Hinduism received +1 for "cyclic creation" of the same kind. Dependent origination and the commentarial niyamas also assert lawfulness without a lawgiver. Under the rule as written the +1 stands.
- Minds: Buddhism does not explain why consciousness exists, but it gives an account of what it is: a beginningless stream of momentary mental events, not derived from matter, analysed in the Abhidharma [9]. Hinduism's "consciousness as fundamental" got +1; naturalism's "future science" got none. Buddhism's account is of Hinduism's kind.
- Obligation: an answer is given (karma and compassion from no-self), contested but present [11][12][13]. Counts.
- Suffering: the Four Noble Truths. The distinctive-resource bonus is well supported across the divide: Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* appendix J quotes the Milindapañha and Vasubandhu and says "Buddha would have agreed" with his reductionism (Hanner 2018 traces the reception) [20]; Flanagan's *The Bodhisattva's Brain* (2011) builds a naturalised Buddhism on the diagnosis [21]; Thompson, a critic, grants the practical core while rejecting "Buddhist exceptionalism" [22].

**Rule applied:** Start at 2. Answers: lawful cosmos by inherited cyclic cosmology (+1); minds by the Abhidharma mind-stream (+1); obligation by karma and compassion (+1); suffering (+1). Distinctive resource, once (+1). Seven.

**Proposed:** keep 7. Strongest reason: the rule's inherited-cosmology clause is explicit and Hinduism was credited under it for the same kind of cosmology. Confidence 50.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to decide that the avyākata refusal overrides the inherited-cosmology clause for Buddhism; then 7→6, and the file's body and score would finally agree with each other.

## Testable claims

**Current:** 4, confidence 60. "Exposure 3: its central claims are about experience, that craving produces suffering and disciplined attention reduces it, which practitioners can test. Held, plus 1: meditation research partly supports them. No failed kind: it promises no answered prayer, and rebirth-memory cases are unconfirmed, which is neutral for every tradition that makes the claim. Four."

**Evidence:**
- Goyal et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine* 2014 (47 RCTs, 3,515 participants): mindfulness programmes showed "moderate evidence" of improved anxiety (d = 0.38 at 8 weeks) and depression (d = 0.30), smaller at 3–6 months; mantra meditation showed low or insufficient evidence; "no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment" [23].
- Van Dam et al., *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 2018, "Mind the Hype": "misinformation and poor methodology associated with past studies of mindfulness may lead public consumers to be harmed, misled, and disappointed"; the field lacks an agreed definition and adequate controls [24].
- Kreplin, Farias and Brazil, *Scientific Reports* 2018: effects of meditation on prosociality are limited to compassion and empathy, and those effects shrink or vanish when the meditation teacher is a study author or when active controls are used [25].
- Britton et al., *Clinical Psychological Science* 2021: in an 8-week mindfulness programme 58% reported a negatively valenced effect and 6–14% lasting adverse effects; Farias et al. 2020 (*Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*) review adverse events across the literature [26][27].
- Net reading: the modest claim practitioners can test, that attention training reduces distress, has held, at effect sizes equal to any active treatment and with a minority harmed. The stronger claims (character transformation, enlightenment) are untested; Thompson notes nirvana "rests on faith" [22]. "Held" is correct only in the weak form; the same weak form earned Hinduism +1, so calibration holds.
- Answered prayer: Lotus Sūtra chapter 25 promises that those who call Avalokiteśvara's name are delivered from fire, flood, demons, bandits and execution "because they call his name" [28]. Reader and Tanabe (*Practically Religious*, 1998) show that this-worldly benefits (genze riyaku) lie "at the very heart" of Japanese Buddhism, including the established sects, and are "justified in scripture and traditional practice" [29]. Theravāda paritta chanting for protection and Pure Land recitation make the same kind of promise. The file's "it promises no answered prayer" describes the early texts, not the tradition; Hinduism's deduction was taken on its devotional strands, and the rule says the prayer trials count "once against every tradition that promises answered prayer."
- Rebirth memory: Stevenson and Tucker's case collection (over 2,000 cases) is observational; critics cite parental shaping, cultural expectation and unreliable recall; Tucker concedes "faulty memories of witnesses are likely present in many cases" [30][31]. Neutral, as the file says.
- Exposure 3 is the top of the defensible range. The core_claim in the file includes "rebirth continues until it does," which is framed as unfalsifiably as the Hindu claims that got Hinduism exposure 1. Separating the experiential core (exposed) from the metaphysics (not) is a fair reading; 2 would also be fair.

**Rule applied:** Exposure 3. Held: meditation research, weak form (+1). Failed: prayer trials, counted once against every tradition that promises answered prayer, which its devotional strands do (−1). Rebirth memory neutral. Three.

**Proposed:** 4→3. Strongest reason: the Lotus Sūtra and the Japanese common religion promise answered prayer in scripture; Hinduism was docked for exactly this and Buddhism was not. Confidence 65.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to hold that only a tradition's earliest layer counts for the prayer rule, and then restore Hinduism's point too.

## Moral fruit

**Current:** 6, confidence 60. "Start at 8: compassion for all sentient beings extends to every creature. Wars and nationalist violence in its name (minus 1); persecution of minorities by Buddhist movements and states in Myanmar and Sri Lanka (minus 1); no exclusion written into its structure. Six."

**Evidence:**
- Wars in its name, confirmed with older roots than the file gives: Mahāvaṃsa 25 has eight arahants tell King Duṭṭhagāmaṇī that his slaughter of Tamils killed only "one and a half human beings," the rest being "unbelievers and men of evil life, not more to be esteemed than beasts"; Deegalle calls this "severely problematic from the point of view of Buddhist doctrines" and notes it has been invoked for centuries to justify killing [32][33]. Brian Victoria's *Zen at War* (1997; 2nd ed. 2006) documents Zen and Pure Land institutional support for Japan's wars from Meiji to 1945, including D. T. Suzuki; critics say he imposes anachronistic standards but not that the documents are wrong [34]. Kittivuddho Bhikkhu's 1976 "Killing Communists Is Not Demeritorious" (Keyes 1978) [35]. One class, one deduction: already taken.
- Persecution and legal coercion by states built on it, confirmed and broadened: pre-1950 Tibet was a monastic-aristocratic state in which, per Goldstein, elites "held most of the land in Tibet in the form of feudal estates" worked by hereditarily bound mi ser; judicial mutilation was in the Sakya legal code until the 13th Dalai Lama banned it in 1913, and whipping remained common (Barnett 2008; French 1995). Coleman, Fjeld and Barnett dispute "serf" as a translation and stress de facto mobility, but not the hereditary obligations or the penal record [36][37][38]. Bhutan, whose constitution makes Buddhism the spiritual heritage of the state, expelled roughly 100,000 Lhotshampa in 1990–93 under the Driglam Namzha code and a citizenship census; Human Rights Watch (2007) documents coerced "voluntary migration forms" [39]. Myanmar's MaBaTha and the 2017 expulsion of over 750,000 Rohingya (Crisis Group 2017) [40]. All fall in the persecution class already deducted; they raise the confidence of that deduction, not the count.
- Exclusion written into its structure: the eight garudhammas (AN 8.51; Cullavagga X), present in every surviving Vinaya, require that a nun of a hundred years' standing bow to a monk ordained that day, forbid nuns to admonish monks, and make nuns' ordination depend on monks. Anālayo finds their historicity "a rather doubtful matter" and the negative passages a later layer, but they are canonical in all schools and governed the order; full ordination for women lapsed in Theravāda and Tibetan Buddhism for about a thousand years, and the revival is contested (Anālayo 2013; Tsedroen 2016) [41][42]. Islam was docked for "the subordinate legal status of women written into traditional law"; this is the same class, written into the tradition's legal code for its own community.
- Weaker candidates in the same class, not counted: Vinaya permission for a monastery to accept gifts of kalpikāra and ārāmika labourers whom Schopen reads as bondmen and slaves, with "no hint of protest or reform" [43] (translation disputed); the Siyam Nikāya's restriction of higher ordination to the Goyigama caste from 1764 [44]; Japanese temples' discriminatory posthumous names for burakumin into the twentieth century (Bodiford 1996) [18]. The last two are practices against the texts, not exclusions written from them.
- The best expression stands: compassion (karuṇā) and loving-kindness (mettā) for all sentient beings, with no in-group condition. Start at 8 is right.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Wars and conquest in its name (−1); persecution and legal coercion by movements and states built on it (−1); an exclusion written into its structure, the subordination of nuns in every Vinaya (−1). Opposition from within (Anālayo, the bhikkhunī revival) does not restore. Five.

**Proposed:** 6→5. Strongest reason: the garudhammas are a textual, code-level subordination of one group that every school carries, the same kind of fact that cost Islam its third point. Confidence 65.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh that the garudhammas bind only the monastic order rather than all women under civil law, and that their attribution to the Buddha is doubted by the tradition's own best scholars.

## Calibration check

1. **Continuous community.** christianity.md: "a continuous, documented community (plus 1)"; judaism.md: "a continuous, documented community of 2,500 years (plus 1)"; hinduism.md: "the tradition is a continuous, documented community for three millennia (plus 1)". buddhism.md credits only the founder. The sangha is documented from the Ashokan edicts (c. 250 BCE) without a break. Reconcile by giving Buddhism the point (5→6).

2. **Answered prayer.** hinduism.md: "Failed, minus 1: prayer trials, counted once against every tradition that promises answered prayer, which its devotional strands do." buddhism.md: "it promises no answered prayer." The Lotus Sūtra's Avalokiteśvara chapter and the Japanese genze riyaku tradition promise deliverance and benefits for calling the name; Theravāda paritta promises protection. Reconcile by applying the deduction to Buddhism (4→3), or by restoring Hinduism's point if only a tradition's earliest layer counts.

3. **Subordination written into law.** islam.md: "the subordinate legal status of women written into traditional law (minus 1)". buddhism.md: "no exclusion written into its structure." The eight garudhammas are the Vinaya's legal subordination of nuns to monks, carried by every school. Reconcile by applying the deduction (6→5), or by narrowing the rule to civil law binding non-members, in which case Islam's point is also open to review.


## Rule problems (not scored)

- **The moral rule charges by tenure of state power.** Judaism (7) and Deism (5, no record) are spared the persecution class largely because neither held a state for most of its history; Buddhism, Christianity, Islam and naturalism each held states and each pays. Example: Tibet's penal mutilation counts against Buddhism; the same acts by a Jewish state of the same period would have had no state to be done by.
- **"Inherited cosmology counts" contradicts "honest refusal is not an answer."** Buddhism both refuses the cosmological questions (avyākata) and carries an inherited cosmology its own leaders now disown. The rule credits the second and ignores the first; the file's body says "silence" while its score says "answered." Example: MN 63 against the Aggañña Sutta.
- **Held/failed is binary; the meditation evidence is not.** Goyal's d ≈ 0.3, equal to any active treatment, with Britton's 6–14% lasting harms, is neither a held nor a failed kind. The rule forces +1 or −1 or silence, and the same evidence credited Hinduism and Buddhism alike.
- **Orality is penalised twice.** A tradition that transmitted orally for four centuries gets no "early, stable text" point and also takes the "late biography" deduction. The two facts have one cause. Example: Buddhism (0, −1) against Islam (+1, −1) on a text fixed within a generation.
- **Same events under two classes.** The file's war and persecution deductions both cite Myanmar and Sri Lanka. Independent evidence exists for each class (Mahāvaṃsa, imperial Japan; Tibet, Bhutan), so both survive, but the file should cite different events for each.
