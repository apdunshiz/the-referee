The research confirms the two deductions Alex is most likely to question. The "return within a generation" deduction survives scrutiny: the majority critical position (Schweitzer, Ehrman, Allison, Fredriksen, Sanders) is that Jesus and Paul expected the end within their generation and it did not come; the strongest counter-reading (Wright, France, Caird: Mark 13 is about Jerusalem's fall in 70 CE) is a real scholarly minority, not apologetics, but it leaves Paul's 1 Thess 4:15 and the early church's own recorded embarrassment (2 Peter 3:4, John 21:23) unexplained. Both are either/or calibration calls: credit Christianity, or remove the point from the other file. Moral and explanation stand.

## Historical footing

**Current:** 6, confidence 65. File's `why`: "Start at 5. Foundations that hold: a well-attested founder and founding event, since Jesus lived, was crucified, and his followers very soon claimed to have seen him alive (plus 1); a continuous, documented community (plus 1). Gone against: the gospels were written decades later by believers and their traditional authorship is doubted by most scholars (minus 1). The resurrection itself is beyond history's reach and neither counts nor costs. Six."

**Evidence:**
- Scholars across the divide accept that 1 Cor 15:3-7 is a pre-Pauline formula Paul "received", dated to within a few years of the crucifixion; Ehrman defines "pre-Pauline" as the window between about 30 and Paul's conversion about 32 CE, and Lüdemann, Wedderburn and Wright put it in the first half of the 30s [26]. Paul's undisputed letters are from the 50s, within about 25 years of the events.
- The New Testament survives in over 5,700 Greek manuscripts; Ehrman's own count of 200,000 to 400,000 variants is the skeptic's headline, but his book concedes the overwhelming majority are spelling and word-order changes with no bearing on meaning [25]. That is a stable text by the standard applied to Islam's Quran in `islam.md`.
- Against: the gospels are anonymous and date roughly 65 to 100 CE; most critical scholars hold the birth narratives, Matthew's risen saints (27:52) and Luke's census under Quirinius to be legend or error. This is the file's existing −1 and the rule's class "biographical sources written late with legend".
- Against: the NT canon's boundary was not settled until the fourth century; "early, stable text" is true of the documents, not of the collection.
- The rule credits "an early, stable text" as a kind separate from founder and community. Islam got it for a text "fixed within about a generation"; Hinduism got it for the Vedas being "among the oldest religious texts". Christianity's `why` neither credits nor debits its text.
- Not scored: the Exodus and patriarchs, which cost Judaism −1. Christianity's founding event is Easter, not Sinai, and its best statement (Catholic and mainline) does not rest on a literal Exodus. See Calibration check.

**Rule applied:** Start at 5. Holds: founder and founding event (+1); continuous documented community (+1); early, stable text, a creed from the 30s, letters from the 50s, and a manuscript record whose variants are catalogued and mostly trivial (+1). Gone against: biographical sources written decades later with legendary material (−1). Seven.

**Proposed:** change 6→7. Strongest reason: the same rule gave Islam +1 for a text fixed within a generation, and Christianity's earliest text is earlier relative to its founding than either Islam's biographies or Buddhism's oral canon. Confidence 60.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh the fourth-century canon boundary as outweighing the first-century stability of the individual documents, and then, for consistency, withdraw the text point from Islam (whose canonical text also has the Sanaa variant) or from Hinduism (whose Vedas were oral for a millennium).

## Internal consistency

**Current:** 5, confidence 60. File's `why`: "Start at 8. Three core doctrines need careful definition to avoid contradiction: the Trinity, the incarnation, and eternal punishment alongside a God defined as love; each has split the tradition. Five. Disagreements between the gospels are a history matter and are not counted here."

**Evidence:**
- Trinity: the rule names it as its own example. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry (Tuggy) frames the "logical problem of the Trinity" and surveys social, Latin, relative-identity and constitution models, none of which commands assent even among Christian philosophers [11]. The SEP's "Philosophy and Christian Theology" says the councils "defined the terminology but did not solve the problem" [12].
- Incarnation: the same SEP entry quotes Richard Cross calling it "the fundamental philosophical problem of Christology": how one person can be both omniscient and limited in knowledge, with the "qua" move, kenotic, compositional and even contradictory (Beall) models on offer [12]. It is a separate definition (Chalcedon 451, two natures one person) from the Trinity (Nicaea 325, three persons one nature), answered a different heresy, and produced a different, still-standing split (the Oriental Orthodox churches). Counting it separately is correct by the rule's letter.
- Hell: the Catholic Catechism 1033-1037 defines hell as "definitive self-exclusion" and insists "God predestines no one to go to hell" [14]; Talbott's SEP entry sets out the inconsistent triad (God wills all saved; God's will is done; some are lost) that every position must deny one leg of [13]; Hart argues the tradition's best statement should be universalism [16]. That the best statement needs this much definition is exactly what the rule deducts for.
- Case for a lower score: the grace-and-freedom problem (Rom 9, Augustine vs Pelagius, Molina vs Báñez, Dort vs Arminius) was argued before a papal commission for nine years (1598-1607) and was "suspended rather than solved": both sides were permitted and forbidden to censure each other [15]. `islam.md` deducts a point for the identical problem ("divine determination alongside human responsibility"). The atonement is also undefined: the SEP notes the church "never formally defined one account of how Christ saves", and the dominant Western models face the objection "why a perfectly merciful God would require satisfaction or punishment" [12].
- Case for a higher score: the tradition's defenders (Rea, Leftow, Swinburne, Stump) treat Trinity and incarnation as one conceptual apparatus (the person/nature distinction) and would count them as one deduction; and the mainline best statement on hell (self-exclusion, Lewis's "doors locked from the inside") is a definition that many philosophers of religion accept as coherent.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Needs careful definition: Trinity (−1), incarnation (−1), eternal punishment alongside a God who is love (−1). Five. Grace and freedom and the atonement are left uncounted, as the file does, but see Calibration check.

**Proposed:** keep 5. Strongest reason: all three deductions are either named in the rule or are a separate conciliar definition with its own schism; neither the "merge Trinity and incarnation" argument nor the "add predestination" argument is stronger than the other, and they cancel. Confidence 60.

**If the owner disagrees:** the one fact to weigh is De Auxiliis. If Islam's determination-and-responsibility deduction stands, the same fact in Christianity's record gives 4; if he thinks the problem is not core for either, Islam goes to 6.

## Explanatory reach

**Current:** 7, confidence 60. File's `why`: "Start at 2. Answers all four: a lawful cosmos and moral obligation through a creator, minds through a soul made in God's image, suffering through a God who undergoes it: plus 4. That last answer is treated by philosophers across the divide as a distinctive resource: plus 1. Its account of animal pain across deep time is weak, but the question is answered. Seven."

**Evidence:**
- Lawful cosmos and minds: Nagel, an atheist, concedes in *Mind and Cosmos* (2012) that theism would account for consciousness and the intelligibility of nature, and rejects it on other grounds [18]. That is the "across the divide" acknowledgement for two of the four questions, which the file does not even use.
- Suffering: the file's distinctive-resource bonus rests on the suffering God (Moltmann, Stump). Stump's *Wandering in Darkness* (2010) is the current best statement, a Thomist theodicy via narrative; a Cambridge *Religious Studies* article identifies three problems with it [17]. The bonus is given once and only requires that the answer be treated as a resource, not that it succeed.
- Against: the problem of evil is treated by non-believing philosophers as the chief objection to theism, not as a Christian strength; the "answer" is the point where the explanation is weakest. The rule, though, scores whether a question is answered, not whether the answer wins.
- Calibration: Buddhism and Hinduism also sit at 7 with the same arithmetic (four answers plus one bonus). Nothing in the rule separates them from Christianity here.

**Rule applied:** Start at 2. Answers: lawful cosmos (+1), minds (+1), obligation (+1), suffering (+1). Distinctive resource, once (+1). Seven.

**Proposed:** keep 7. Strongest reason: 7 is the rule's ceiling for any worldview that answers all four, and Christianity does. Confidence 70.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to rule that "a God who undergoes suffering" is a response rather than an explanation, which would also strip Buddhism's "diagnosis" bonus.

## Testable claims

**Current:** 2, confidence 55. File's `why`: "Exposure 4: a body or a confession could have refuted the resurrection, the most exposed central claim of any major faith. Its surrounding facts are foundation claims scored under history, not again here. Failed, minus 1 each: the apparent prediction of a return within a generation; prayer trials, counted once against every tradition that promises answered prayer. Two."

**Evidence on the parousia deduction:**
- The texts: Mark 9:1, 13:30, Matt 10:23, 1 Thess 4:15-17 ("we who are alive"), 1 Cor 7:29, Rom 13:11. The early church recorded the problem itself: 2 Peter 3:4 answers scoffers asking "where is the promise of his coming?", and John 21:23 corrects a rumour that the beloved disciple would not die before the return. A UBS translators' handbook notes the delay "created tremendous problems for the early church, as can be seen in ... Thessalonians" (Arichea and Nida, UBS Handbook on 2 Peter, 1980).
- Majority critical reading: Schweitzer (1906), Ehrman (*Jesus: Apocalyptic Prophet*, 1999) and Allison (*Jesus of Nazareth: Millenarian Prophet*, 1998; *Constructing Jesus*, 2010) hold that Jesus expected the end within his generation. Allison, a believing scholar, concludes that this makes Jesus "either a failed or a false prophet" unless one reads him as a prophet whose timing was off, and that "most of what Jesus envisaged in his eschatological prophecies cannot be identified with past events" [1][2]. Ehrman documents how later gospels soften the sayings as the generation died [3].
- The preterist counter: Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) and France (*Mark*, NIGTC 2002) read Mark 13 as "exclusively about the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 AD", with "the coming of the Son of Man" as Daniel 7 enthronement language, so "this generation" was fulfilled [4][5 Bird]. Michael Bird sides with Wright but concedes Matthew "conflates AD 70 with the second coming" and that Allison, Adams and Sloan disagree [5].
- Response to the counter: Adams, *Tyndale Bulletin* 56.2 (2005), argues from Mark's own usage (8:38, 13:24-27, 14:62) that the Son of Man sayings refer to the parousia, not vindication, and that Wright's reading is eisegesis [6]; Robert Stein's commentary grants Wright some verses but says he "does not do justice" to Mark's judgment-and-glory language (Stein, *Mark*, BECNT 2008). Even on Wright's reading, Paul's "we who are alive" remains. Christian scholars who accept the problem now argue the prophecy was conditional (Hays ed., *When the Son of Man Didn't Come*, 2016) [7]; C. S. Lewis called Mark 13:30 "the most embarrassing verse in the Bible" and accepted that at face value Jesus was wrong about the date [8].
- Evidence on prayer trials: STEP (Benson et al., 2006, 1,802 bypass patients) found no effect of intercessory prayer and more complications in patients who knew they were prayed for [9]; the Cochrane review (Roberts et al., 2009, 10 trials, about 7,600 participants) found the evidence equivocal and recommended no further trials [10]. The deduction is levied identically on every tradition that promises answered prayer, so it never changes the ranking.
- Held kinds the file does not score: Judaism gets +1 for "the people's survival and return to the land, a promise rather than a foundation claim". Christianity's texts promise that the church would not be overcome (Matt 16:18) and that the message would reach all nations (Matt 24:14, Acts 1:8). Both are promises, not foundation claims, and both held. The counter is that a church's survival is a weaker and more self-fulfilling prediction than a nation's return after 1,900 years; but the rule scores "kinds", and the kind is the same. The temple-destruction prediction (Mark 13:2) is neutral: Mark is dated too close to 70 to settle whether it was prophecy or report.
- Exposure: 4 is the right order of magnitude. The resurrection was exposed in its first decade (a body, a recantation) but is no longer; naturalism's claim (no confirmed miracle) is exposed every day. 4 for both treats a historically exposed claim and a currently exposed one alike; see Rule problems.

**Rule applied:** Exposure 4. Held: the promise that the community would endure and spread, a promise rather than a foundation claim, as in `judaism.md` (+1). Failed: the return within a generation, the majority critical reading, with the church's own canonical texts recording the embarrassment (−1); prayer trials, once, as for every tradition promising answered prayer (−1). Three.

**Proposed:** change 2→3. Strongest reason: calibration with Judaism's +1 for a held promise; the parousia deduction is kept because the preterist reading, though scholarly, is a minority that does not cover Paul or 2 Peter. Confidence 50 on the change; 65 on keeping the parousia deduction.

**If the owner disagrees:** the one fact is whether Matt 16:18 and 24:14 are a checkable promise of the same kind as Judaism's survival and return. If not, Christianity stays at 2 and, for consistency, Judaism's held point should be re-examined on the same ground (it was also a community predicting its own persistence).

## Moral fruit

Wars and conquest in its name, the crusades (minus 1); persecution of dissenters, the inquisitions (minus 1); slavery sanctioned from its texts for centuries (minus 1), each opposed from within by appeal to the same texts, which the rule does not restore. **Evidence:**
- Wars: the crusades were preached by popes with indulgences attached; this is conquest in its name on any historian's account (Riley-Smith, *The Crusades: A History*, 3rd ed. 2014).
- Persecution: Kamen's revisionist *Spanish Inquisition* (1998) lowers the executions to about 3,000 over three centuries, most in the first forty years [20]. Lower numbers do not remove the class: legal coercion of dissenters and converts is still present in the record, and the rule counts presence, not scale.
- Slavery: Noll, *The Civil War as a Theological Crisis* (2006), shows the proslavery argument was the plain reading of texts that "never condemn slavery" and regulate it in both testaments, and that antislavery readers had to appeal to the "spirit" of the gospel [19]. "Sanctioned from its texts" is accurate for the record of states built on it.
- Case for higher: none under the rule; all three classes are present, and the rule says opposition from within does not restore a point. Christianity's internal abolitionism (Wilberforce, the Quakers) is the strongest case of a tradition reversing its own record, which the rule deliberately does not credit.
- Case for lower: none; three deductions is the rule's maximum.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Wars and conquest in its name (−1); persecution and legal coercion (−1); slavery sanctioned from its texts (−1). Five.

**Proposed:** keep 5. Strongest reason: all three classes are present and the rule has no further deductions. Confidence 75.

**If the owner disagrees:** the only lever is the rule itself (see Rule problems), not a fact.

## Calibration check

1. **Early text.** `islam.md`: "a text fixed within about a generation and transmitted with unusual stability ... (plus 1)". `hinduism.md`: "the Vedas are among the oldest religious texts (plus 1)". `christianity.md` never mentions its text. The 1 Cor 15 creed (30s) and Paul's letters (50s) are closer to the founding than the Quran's codification is to Muhammad's death, and the manuscript record is larger and better catalogued. Reconcile by crediting Christianity (+1) or by withdrawing the point from Islam, whose canonical text has the Sanaa variant the file itself mentions.

2. **Held promise.** `judaism.md` testability: "Held, plus 1: the people's survival and return to the land, a promise rather than a foundation claim." Christianity's survival and spread (Matt 16:18, 24:14) is the same kind. Reconcile by crediting both or neither.

3. **Grace and freedom.** `islam.md` consistency: "divine determination alongside human responsibility, debated by its own schools for centuries (minus 1)". Christianity's De Auxiliis controversy and Dort are the same problem, debated as long, and left undefined by Rome [15]. `christianity.md` does not count it. Reconcile by deducting from both (Christianity 4) or from neither (Islam 6).

4. **Structural exclusion, affecting the two worldviews Alex asked about.** `buddhism.md` moral: "no exclusion written into its structure." Silk's *Encyclopedia of Buddhism* entry reports "copious inscriptional and documentary evidence for the institutional monastic ownership of slaves from Sri Lanka, Cambodia, Burma, Thailand, Korea, China, and Japan", the Vinaya bars slaves from ordination, and Gombrich notes endowing monasteries with slaves was meritorious [22]; the eight garudhammas subordinate every nun to every monk. `hinduism.md` moral deducts nothing for persecution or legal coercion, yet Nepal (a Hindu kingdom until 2008) and seven Indian states criminalise conversion away from Hinduism, enforced asymmetrically per USCIRF [23], and Verardi argues (contested) for Brahmanical violence against Buddhists [24]. By the standard applied to Christianity (presence of the class, not scale), Buddhism's slavery and Hinduism's anti-conversion laws each cost a point. That alone would bring Buddhism to 28 and Hinduism to 25.

## Rule problems (not scored)

- **Moral has a floor set by the length of state power.** Any tradition that governed for over a millennium has all three classes, so Christianity and Islam sit at the floor (5) while Deism, with "no communities and no record", also scores 5, and Judaism's 7 is explicitly credited to its having been "a minority faith for most of its history". The rule measures opportunity, not ethic. Example: Christianity's abolitionism, the clearest case of a tradition reversing its own record, is worth exactly nothing.
- **Exposure conflates past and present exposure.** The resurrection was refutable for a decade; naturalism's "no confirmed miracle" is refutable now. Both score 4. Either historical exposure should score lower, or the resurrection's unmatched exposure in its own time should score higher; 4 for both is the one answer that is wrong for one of them.
- **The prayer-trials deduction never discriminates.** It is levied on every theist tradition identically, so it only moves everyone down together. It also tests a claim (strangers' prayer improves surgical outcomes at a statistically detectable rate) that the Catechism's own best statement (2735-2737, on why prayer is not "answered" mechanically) does not make. Naturalism is additionally credited +1 for the same null result, so one experiment moves the gap by two points.
- **Consistency rewards vagueness.** A tradition that has held councils and defined its doctrines (Christianity, three deductions) is penalised more than one that left the same questions open (Buddhism, one deduction, though the ontological status of nirvana, the mechanism of karma without an agent, and the bodhisattva-versus-arhat split would each qualify as "needs careful definition"). Hinduism was penalised for having too many answers; Buddhism for too few of its problems being named.
- **A possible double count between history and testability.** The parousia deduction is drawn from gospel texts whose late composition already cost a point under history, and the preterist dispute is in part about what those texts originally meant, a history question. The rule's "foundation claims already scored under history are not scored again" could be read to cover it. The report keeps the deduction because the claim is a prediction, not a foundation claim.
