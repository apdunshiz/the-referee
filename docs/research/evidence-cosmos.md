Researcher: Opus 5.5. Read-only on the repo. Draft rule applied exactly as written; proposed changes to the rule are only in "Rule problems".

The biggest new input is specialist survey data that the evidence files don't use yet. In the Big Mysteries Survey (run in 2025, published in 2026; 1,675 researchers and enthusiasts responding through the APS's *Physics Magazine*), 68.4% said "the Big Bang" names evolution from a hot, dense state and says nothing about an absolute beginning; only 24.0% read it as an absolute beginning [1][2]. A 2025 survey of 85 conference physicists in Copenhagen gave the same 68% [3]. So the kalam's second premise is a minority view among the people who study it, and for-01 falls by one. On fine-tuning, the same survey found brute fact 26.0%, multiverse 19.9%, a deeper principle 15.7% and a designer 8.9% [1], and philosophers in the 2020 PhilPapers survey gave a similar spread [4]. That confirms the sensitivity as mainstream and design as a minority reading. The rule still gives for-02 a 7, but on thin support: clause (b) rests on one book by one atheist philosopher (Monton 2009) as described by one review, and two judgment calls ((b) and (f)) each separate 7 from 6. Under my reading, (f) applies only when the argument, with every premise granted, delivers something short of a personal being (for side) or short of "no God" (against side); a designer is already personal, so (f) does not apply to for-02. for-03 falls by one because the principle it needs, the principle of sufficient reason, is out of favour even by its defenders' account. for-06 and against-05 stay at 4 on the rule, and the research mainly corrects their wording. With my proposals and every other group's lines held fixed, the headline goes from 42/84 (50.0%) to 40/82 (48.8%).

## for-01: The universe seems to have had a beginning

**Current:** strength 6, confidence 80. Status: "Most cosmologists treat a hot, dense early state as settled and an absolute beginning as open; philosophers are split on whether a beginning needs a cause at all."

**Evidence:**
- Big Mysteries Survey (run 2025, published 2026), 1,675 respondents. "The big bang" was read as an absolute beginning with a singularity by 19.5%, as an absolute beginning without one by 4.5%, and as "a theory of evolution from a hot, dense state, saying nothing about whether there was an absolute beginning" by 68.4% [1][2]. About 20% of respondents were enthusiasts and 30% worked in "other science". The public dashboard [1b] did not render a field breakdown, so no specialist-only figure is available.
- Copenhagen Survey (2025): 68% of 85 physicists at a black-hole conference chose "evolved from a hot dense state" over "an absolute beginning time" [3].
- The Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem proves that past-directed geodesics are incomplete in any classical spacetime whose average expansion rate is positive [5]. Mithani and Vilenkin argue that the three main escapes all fail: eternal inflation, cyclic models and the emergent "cosmic egg" [6]. Vilenkin: "All the evidence we have says that the universe had a beginning" (New Scientist 2012, as reproduced in [8]; see also [7]). This is the case that 6 is too low.
- The other direction:
  - Guth, a co-author of the theorem, "suspect[s] the universe didn't have a beginning", as Carroll reports from the 2014 debate [9].
  - Susskind argues that inflation puts any beginning effectively at minus infinity [10].
  - Geshnizjani, Ling and Quintin (2023) show that the BGV boundary is only a coordinate artefact in some cases, such as negligible matter, so spacetime can continue past it. But the early universe is thought to have been matter-dominated, and in that case the boundary is a real curvature singularity where the laws of gravity break down [11][12]. That half of the result supports a genuine edge.
- The SEP "Cosmology and Theology" (Halvorson and Kragh, rev. 2021) says big bang cosmology gives "prima facie support for theism". It also says "it is an empirical, rather than a metaphysical, question whether the big bang was the beginning of the universe", and that loop and string cosmologies allow a pre-big-bang phase [13].
- The SEP "Cosmological Argument" (Reichenbach, rev. 2026) treats the kalam as live and contested. It reports Morriston's view that the causal premise is "neither intuitive nor supported" at the boundary of time, Oppy's verdict that "there is no persuasive cosmological argument", and that defenders such as Craig and Loke are still developing it [14].
- Morriston (2000) argues that even granting a cause, nothing shows it is a person. Craig replied in 2002 [15].
- Carroll (2014) argues that "cause" is not fundamental-physics vocabulary and that models such as Hartle-Hawking need no outside cause [9].

**Rule applied:** Key datum: the finite age of the hot big bang plus BGV past-incompleteness for classical expanding spacetimes. The premise the argument needs is that the universe began to exist absolutely. I score these as separate items, the same way for-02 separates "sensitivity" from "needs explanation".
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the hot big bang 13.8 billion years ago is textbook cosmology, and BGV is a proved theorem (+1).
- (b) does not hold: I found no named leading non-theist who grants that a beginning is some evidence for God. Halvorson, who co-wrote the "prima facie" line, is a theist. Carroll and Guth deny it.
- (c) holds: the SEP entry treats the kalam as live and contested, not refuted [14] (+1).
- (d) holds: "the universe began to exist" is held by 24% of respondents against 68% who say the Big Bang implies nothing either way [1][3] (−1).
- (e) does not hold: unlike fine-tuning, where physicists were asked "what explains it?" and 42% chose a specific rival, no survey or reference work shows specialists accepting a particular bounce, no-boundary or eternal-past model as an adequate account of the boundary. The 68% is agnosticism about a beginning, already counted under (d).
- (f) holds: getting from "a cause" to "a personal God" needs a further argument, and Morriston contests it [15] (−1).
- Five.

**Proposed:** change 6→5. Single strongest reason: the premise that the universe began is a minority view among physicists (24% vs 68%). Confidence 65.

**Text fixes:**
- status (286 chars): "Physicists treat a hot, dense early state as settled; in a survey of 1,675 researchers and enthusiasts (APS Physics Magazine, 2026), 68% said the Big Bang implies nothing about an absolute beginning and 24% that it marks one. Philosophers are split on whether a beginning needs a cause."
- best_reply: "any universe expanding on average cannot be extended infinitely into the past" overstates the theorem. BGV is about geodesics in a classical spacetime, and [11] shows the boundary can sometimes be crossed. Replacement (463 chars): "The Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem shows that in any classical spacetime that has expanded on average, past-directed paths run out after a finite time, so the expanding phase had a past boundary; Vilenkin argues the main escapes (eternal inflation, cycles, an emergent "cosmic egg") fail, though co-author Guth suspects there was no beginning. And a timeless cause producing a first moment is at least coherent, which is all the argument needs to get off the ground."
- body, paragraph 2: replace "the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem says that a universe that has been expanding on average cannot have an infinite past. That is suggestive, not decisive: the theorem assumes classical spacetime," with "the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem says that in a universe that has been expanding on average, the expanding phase cannot reach infinitely far into the past. That is suggestive, not decisive: the theorem assumes classical spacetime and does not say what, if anything, lies beyond that boundary,"

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to treat the 68% "says nothing about a beginning" answer as agnosticism rather than disagreement, so (d) does not fire. That keeps 6. If he instead treats the absolute beginning as the key datum, (a) also fails and the line falls to 4. See Rule problems.

## for-02: The constants of physics sit in a narrow life-permitting range

**Current:** strength 7, confidence 80. Status: "The sensitivity itself is mainstream physics; what it means is unresolved, with physicists split between multiverse explanations, undiscovered deeper laws, and design."

**Evidence:**
- Big Mysteries Survey (2026). The question took the sensitivity as given and asked what explains it: brute facts 26.0%, multiverse 19.9%, a principle such as naturalness 15.7%, intelligent designer 8.9%, cosmic Darwinism 6.2%, other 10.6%, no opinion 12.6% [1]. Copenhagen's plurality was also brute fact, at 33% [1][3].
- PhilPapers 2020, fine-tuning question (n=807): design 17%, multiverse 15%, brute fact 32%, no fine-tuning 22%. Among philosophers of physical science (n=16): design 13%, multiverse 25%, brute fact 38%, no fine-tuning 6% [4]. These figures come from a secondary tabulation; the results page refused automated access.
- Barnes (2012) reviews the physics literature and finds the sensitivity robust. He defends no theological conclusion and notes serious problems for the multiverse [16]. The SEP "Fine-Tuning" (Friederich, rev. 2026) says the life-permitting character depends "delicately" on the constants "according to many physicists". It adds that "many regard the argument from fine-tuning for a designer as the strongest version of the teleological argument" [17].
- Monton, an atheist philosopher of physics, counts fine-tuning among the "somewhat plausible" design arguments in *Seeking God in Science* (2009). Sarkar's NDPR review reports that these arguments left him "less certain of his atheism" [18]. This one book, as described by one review, is the only grant I found. His 2006 BJPS paper cuts the other way: it argues, via the problem of old evidence, that a reasonable agent can hold opinions on which fine-tuning gives no boost to the probability of God [19].
- The case that 7 is too high:
  - Adams (2019, *Physics Reports*): "viable universes exist over a range of parameter space", and other astrophysical processes could support life [20]. Stenger argued the same at a popular level [21].
  - Colyvan, Garfield and Priest (2005) and McGrew, McGrew and Vestrup (2001) argue that with no normalisable probability over infinite ranges, "improbable" may have no defined meaning [22][23]. The SEP notes that a uniform measure "assigns zero probability to any finite interval" [17].
- Likelihood reversal. Carroll (2014; 2019): "God could make life no matter what the laws of physics were. Fine tuning is only necessary if life is purely physical and God doesn't exist" [9][24]. Halvorson, a Christian philosopher at Princeton, argues that fine-tuning does not imply a fine-tuner [25]. Both are steelmen for "too high" from both sides.

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the sensitivity is standard physics. Only 6% of philosophers of physics deny it [4], and the physicists' survey took it as its premise [1] (+1).
- (b) holds, narrowly: Monton, a named atheist philosopher of physics, grants the design arguments some force in his 2009 book [18]. His own 2006 paper [19] argues a reasonable agent need not be moved, so this grant is thin. Carroll calls it the best theist argument but argues it counts against theism, so he is not a grant (+1).
- (c) holds: SEP "Fine-Tuning" and SEP "Teleological Arguments" treat it as the live, strongest design argument [17][26] (+1).
- (d) does not hold: the premise that the constants need explanation is the majority view. Brute fact is a plurality, not a majority (26% of physicists, 32% of philosophers). The normalisation worry is contested, not a settled specialist consensus.
- (e) holds: non-design physical explanations (multiverse, naturalness, cosmic Darwinism) together draw 42% of physicists [1] (−1).
- (f) does not hold under my test: granting the premises yields a designer, which is already a personal agent.
- Seven.

**Proposed:** keep 7. Single strongest reason: the sensitivity is mainstream and both SEP entries treat the argument as live, with one named non-theist conceding some force, while design is still a 9% minority among physicists. Confidence 50: (b) rests on one book as described by one review.

**Text fixes:**
- status: "split between multiverse explanations, undiscovered deeper laws, and design" leaves out brute fact, the plurality answer. Replacement (254 chars): "The sensitivity itself is mainstream physics; what it means is unresolved. In a survey of 1,675 researchers and enthusiasts (APS Physics Magazine, 2026), 26% called the values brute facts, 20% chose a multiverse, 16% a deeper principle and 9% a designer."
- body, paragraph 2: "Three answers are on the table" leaves out the most common answer. Change to "Four answers are on the table". After the "deeper theory" sentence, add: "Or the values are simply brute facts, the most common answer among physicists and philosophers surveyed." Add to the costs sentence: "a brute fact gives up on explanation."
- best_objection (optional, 368 chars), adds the reversal that is now the main scholarly objection: "Without knowing what values the constants could have taken, or how probable each is, "narrow" has no defined meaning; if there are many universes, we would necessarily find ourselves in one that permits observers; and an all-powerful God could make life under any physics, so finely tuned physics is what you would expect if life is purely physical, not if God exists."

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh one atheist's partial concession (Monton) as not enough for (b). That makes it 6. Alternatively he could count "designer to God" as a contested step under (f), which also gives 6. Both together give 5.

## for-03: Something exists rather than nothing, and the universe does not explain itself

**Current:** strength 6, confidence 70. Status: "A central argument in philosophy of religion since Leibniz; it stands or falls on whether every contingent fact needs an explanation, which philosophers dispute."

**Evidence:**
- SEP "Cosmological Argument" (Reichenbach, rev. 2026): the key premise is that "all contingent beings have a fully adequate or complete explanation". Van Inwagen "argues that the PSR must be rejected", because explaining the conjunction of all contingent truths collapses contingency. Pruss replies by separating explanatory sufficiency from logical sufficiency [14]. The argument is live and actively defended (Pruss, Gale and Pruss).
- SEP "Principle of Sufficient Reason" (Melamed and Lin, rev. 2023): the principle has "strong intuitive appeal", but many find it "too bold and expensive". It is presented as a live research topic (Dasgupta, Amijee), not refuted [27].
- Della Rocca, who defends the principle, lamented that it had "fallen on hard times", as Lu-Adler reports [38]. His 2010 paper argues that no principled line can be drawn short of full PSR [28]. This is a defender's admission that the principle is unfashionable, not survey data. It is the case that 6 is too low only if the owner finds the no-arbitrary-line argument compelling.
- The gap to God, per the SEP:
  - The Gale-Pruss weak-PSR argument "tells little about [the being's] power, goodness, and other qualities".
  - Rundle identifies the necessary being with matter or energy.
  - Oppy argues that a necessary initial physical state does as well as God [14].
- Big Mysteries Survey: 15.7% of physicists expect the constants to be "set by a principle such as naturalness" [1]. So a minority hopes physics will show some values could not have been otherwise. This matters to the file's claim that physics treats the laws as contingent.

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds, with weak evidence: the key datum, that the physical world is contingent and current physics leaves its laws and constants unexplained, is the standard view. Necessitarian views (Oppy's necessary initial state, Spinozism) are minority positions. No survey exists (+1).
- (b) does not hold: Oppy grants contingency but deny that it favours theism. I found no named non-theist who grants it some evidential weight.
- (c) holds: SEP "Cosmological Argument" treats the Leibnizian argument as live and contested [14] (+1).
- (d) holds, with weak evidence: full PSR is needed and is out of favour among metaphysicians. The SEP says "too bold and expensive" and a defender says "fallen on hard times" [27][38]. No survey (−1).
- (e) does not hold: "brute fact" is a refusal to explain, not a rival explanation. I score it this way here and in for-01. No rival explanation is accepted as adequate by many.
- (f) holds: getting from "a necessary being" to a personal God is a further step, and the SEP says it is contested [14] (−1).
- Five.

**Proposed:** change 6→5. Single strongest reason: the argument needs the strong PSR, which most metaphysicians reject, and even its success yields only a necessary being. Confidence 55: no survey bears on (a) or (d).

**Text fixes:**
- body, paragraph 3: "the laws and constants are described as contingent by the same scientists who study them" overstates. Physicists say current theory does not fix the values, and some hope a deeper principle will. Replacement: "current physics leaves the values of the laws and constants unexplained and gives no sign that they hold of necessity."
- body, last paragraph: "the disagreement is about one premise" is fine as written. The file already flags the step from a necessary being to a mind. No change.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to judge the PSR a live mainstream option rather than a minority one. Della Rocca's argument and Pruss's book are the case for that, and it would remove (d) and keep 6.

## for-06: Nature is intelligible, and mathematics describes it uncannily well

**Current:** strength 4, confidence 60. Status: "The applicability of mathematics is a recognised puzzle, but it has several non-theistic treatments and few philosophers treat it as a strong argument on its own."

**Evidence:**
- Wigner (1960): the fit is "a wonderful gift which we neither understand nor deserve". He drew no theological conclusion [29].
- Hamming (1980) offered four non-theistic partial explanations: "We see what we look for", "We select the kind of mathematics to use", "Science in fact answers comparatively few problems", and "The evolution of man provided the model". He then conceded that "all of the explanations I have given when added together simply are not enough" [30]. This is the strongest case that 4 is too low.
- Steiner (1998) argues that the success of "Pythagorean" and "formalist" discovery strategies is "a significant challenge to naturalism itself". This is an argument against naturalism, not for God (IEP, Pincock [31]). Critics (Simons; Bangu 2006) say his examples don't support his premises [31][32].
- The IEP entry (Pincock) presents applicability as ranging "from the completely trivial to the utterly mysterious". It recommends a "piecemeal approach", offers structuralist accounts as answers to Steiner, and discusses no theistic conclusion [31].
- The SEP "Teleological Arguments" (Ratzsch and Koperski, rev. 2023) lists "the intelligibility of nature" among "problematic proposals" that are "empirically further removed" [26]. This is the strongest case that 4 is not too low.
- Theistic statement: Craig's "The Argument from the Applicability of Mathematics" (advocacy, Reasonable Faith [33]) argues that a common rational source explains the fit better than naturalistic alternatives.

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: that mathematics describes physical law with great precision, often using structures developed for their own sake, is uncontroversial [29][31]. Whether that is "uncanny" is the interpretation (+1).
- (b) does not hold: I found no named non-theist who grants it as evidence for God. Wigner and Hamming leave it open, and Steiner is not a non-theist conceding the point.
- (c) does not hold: the SEP calls intelligibility "problematic", and the IEP does not discuss a theistic argument.
- (d) not established: no survey shows a needed premise to be a minority view.
- (e) holds, weakly: selection-effect and structuralist accounts (Hamming's items 1 to 3; Pincock's structuralism) are standard non-theistic treatments in philosophy of mathematics. Hamming himself judged his account insufficient (−1).
- (f) holds: the best non-theist-friendly version (Steiner) yields only that naturalism is under strain. Getting from "the world is user-friendly to minds" to a personal creator is a further contested step (Platonist and Pythagorean alternatives) (−1).
- Four.

**Proposed:** keep 4. Single strongest reason: no standard reference work treats it as a live theistic argument. Confidence 50: (e) is close.

**Text fixes:** none required. The status is now supported by the SEP's "problematic proposals" line. Optional precision in body paragraph 3: "Some physicists suggest..." could read "Hamming suggested, then judged insufficient, that..." to reflect [30].

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh Hamming's own admission, that naturalistic explanations "are not enough", as defeating (e). That gives 5.

## against-05: The universe is vast, old and almost entirely hostile to life

**Current:** strength 4, confidence 65. Status: "A common intuition more than a developed argument; most philosophers on both sides consider it weak on its own, since nothing about theism as philosophers state it predicts a small universe, whatever traditional readings of scripture assumed."

**Evidence:**
- Everitt (2004, chapter 11) frames it as an argument from scale: a God with the purposes traditionally ascribed would make a human-scale universe, and the actual one is not human-scale. In his words, "the findings of modern science significantly reduce the probability that theism is true" (quoted in [34]).
- Emily Thomas (Durham, 2017) finds theistic replies "a million miles away from fully explaining why", and says the vastness "seem[s] to sweep us towards atheism" [34]. This is the case that 4 is too low, from a professional philosopher.
- Dicke (1961, *Nature*): the universe's age is set by the time needed to make carbon ("carbon is required to make physicists"). In an expanding universe, age fixes the scale [35]. Collins makes the same point, that a life-permitting universe must be large and old [39]. This is the strongest case that the datum has an accepted rival explanation.
- Dumsday, a theist, treats the related "delayed life" objection as serious enough to answer in print (*Sophia* 2017). He argues that the cosmos may be tuned for the possibility of life rather than for life (*Faith and Philosophy* 2019) [36][37].
- Carroll's reversal applies here too. If God could make life anywhere, a universe hostile almost everywhere is what naturalism predicts [24]. That links this line to for-02.
- I found no SEP or Oxford/Cambridge handbook entry on the argument from scale. Its literature is Everitt, Martin, Sagan, Stenger and replies in journals and on blogs.

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the size, age and emptiness of the universe are mainstream cosmology (+1).
- (b) does not hold: I found no named leading theist who grants it as evidence against God. Dumsday engages it and rejects it.
- (c) does not hold: no standard reference work treats it as a live argument.
- (d) not established: the premise that theism predicts a human-scale universe is contested. No survey shows it to be a minority view, and the file's "most philosophers" claim is unsourced.
- (e) holds: Dicke's observer-selection point, that a life-bearing universe under our physics must be billions of years old and correspondingly large, is accepted physics [35] (−1).
- (f) holds: getting from "not built mainly for humans" to "no God" needs a further premise about God's purposes, and that premise is contested [36][37] (−1).
- Four.

**Proposed:** keep 4. Single strongest reason: physics explains why any life-bearing universe like ours is vast and old. Confidence 60.

**Text fixes:**
- status: "most philosophers on both sides consider it weak" is unsourced, and a 2017 Durham philosopher takes the opposite view [34]. Replacement (246 chars): "A common intuition more than a developed argument. Theist philosophers answer that nothing in theism predicts a small universe and that life needs a vast, old one; some philosophers (Everitt, Thomas) count it against theism as traditionally held."
- body, last sentence: "It belongs on the list because people find it moving, not because philosophers rate it highly." Replacement: "It belongs on the list because people find it moving; few philosophers have developed it at length, and its best form targets ancient cosmologies more than God."

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh Dicke's point as answering only the age and size, not the hostility. Most of the universe being lethal is not needed for carbon. That would drop (e) and give 5.

## Double counting

**for-02 vs against-05 (fine-tuning vs a vast, hostile universe): distinct data that interact.**
- The data differ. for-02 is the values of the constants. against-05 is the scale, age and emptiness of the universe, given those values.
- They are linked in two ways:
  - The theist reply to against-05 (Dicke [35], Collins [39]) uses for-02's physics. Granting that our physics is life-permitting partly explains the vastness, which is why against-05 takes (e).
  - Carroll's reversal [24] uses both data to argue that a life-hostile, physically tuned cosmos is what naturalism predicts.
- No merge is needed, but if the owner merged against-05 into for-02 as a qualifier, the against column would lose 4. Headline: 42/(42+38) = 52.5% now, or 40/78 = 51.3% with my proposals. A one-sided merge moves the percentage for structural reasons, because the ratio compares column sums. It does not mean the "for" case got stronger.

**for-01 vs for-03 (beginning vs contingency): distinct.**
- for-01's datum is physical: the finite past of the expanding phase. for-03's datum is metaphysical: the contingency of whatever exists. for-03 is built to work for a beginningless universe, and its file says so.
- The two lines do share a conclusion type (a cause or necessary ground), and they share the (f) gap.
- If for-01 were merged into for-03 as one "cosmological argument" line at 6, the for column would lose 6 now (6+6 replaced by 6), giving 36/78 = 46.2%. With my proposals it would lose 4 (5+5 replaced by 6), giving 36/78 = 46.2%. Again, the move is structural: one side loses a line and the other doesn't.

The other pairs in the brief (for-05/against-07, for-07/against-03, against-02/06/08) don't touch my lines.

## Rule problems (not scored)

1. **"Key datum" is undefined, and the choice swings a line by 2.**
   - For for-01, take the key datum to be "an absolute beginning". Then the survey both fails (a) and fires (d), and the line scores 4.
   - Take it to be "the hot big bang plus the BGV theorem", with the beginning as a needed premise. Then (a) holds, (d) fires, and the line scores 5.
   - Suggested wording: "(a) the observation or result the argument starts from (not its interpretation) is mainstream in the relevant field; (d) a premise needed to get from that datum to the conclusion is a minority view."

2. **(f) has no stated threshold.** The example ("a cause" to "a personal God") does not say whether "a designer" to "God" also counts. That one choice moves for-02 between 7 and 6. Suggested wording: "(f) granting every premise, the argument yields something short of a personal being (for the for column) or short of 'no God' (for the against column)."

3. **(b) and (c) are asymmetric across columns.**
   - Reference works cover arguments for God (cosmological, teleological) as named entries far more than popular arguments against God (scale, retreat of gaps). So (c) structurally favours the for column.
   - (b) depends on whether a single named concession (Monton) counts as "leading defenders".
   - Suggested wording: "(b) at least one named, published scholar on the other side grants that the datum is some evidence for this side, in a peer-reviewed or university-press work." Also: "(c) a standard reference work or handbook discusses the argument and does not call it refuted; silence counts as not holding."

4. **(d) and (e) need survey data that mostly doesn't exist.** No survey covers the PSR, the argument from scale, or the applicability of mathematics. In those lines I marked a clause "not established" rather than holding. Suggested addition: "Where no survey or reference-work statement exists, (d) and (e) do not hold." Without that rule, scores depend on the researcher's impressions.

5. **The brute-fact problem.** Surveys put "brute fact" as the plurality answer on fine-tuning. The rule does not say whether brute fact is a "rival explanation" under (e). I treated it as not one, in both for-02 and for-03. Suggested addition: "A refusal to explain is not a rival explanation."

6. **Survey populations.** The largest physicists' survey [1] includes about 20% enthusiasts and 30% from "other science", and no professional-only breakdown is published. The rule should say whether such surveys qualify as specialist data.
