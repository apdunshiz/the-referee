# Evidence report: group "evil" (against-01, against-02, against-06, against-08)

Model: Claude Opus 5.5. Read-only on the repo; nothing under the-referee was edited.

**Two readings applied to all four lines.**

1. **One disagreement, one charge.** A single disagreement is charged once, even when it fits more than one of (d), (e) and (f).
   - (d) is a premise the argument needs.
   - (e) is a positive account of why the datum occurs that works without the line's conclusion, judged on the plain text: named specialists in the field accept it as adequate.
   - (f) is the gap between what the argument concludes and "no God".
   - If the (e) account is only the denial of the (d) premise, it is charged once.
   - A reply that denies the datum is evidence without explaining it (skeptical theism, Davies's "God is not a moral agent") is not an (e) explanation. Where it bears on scope, it counts under (f).
2. **What "God" means for (f).** The site scores deism as a God-belief, and the for-column charges (f) when an argument stops short of a personal God. To match that, an against-line takes (f) when it rules out only a perfectly good, perfectly loving or intervening God, and leaves an indifferent or non-intervening creator standing.

## against-01: The amount and distribution of suffering

**Current:** strength 7, confidence 80. Status: "The strongest and most discussed argument against God; the logical version (evil and God are incompatible) is widely considered answered, while the evidential version (the actual amount of evil is strong evidence against God) remains the main live objection."

**Evidence:**
- **Swinburne grants the direction (toward higher).** Swinburne concludes that "evil provides a good C-inductive argument against the existence of God. But it does not provide a very strong one". In his notation, P(God | evil & k) < P(God | k), "but the former is not less than the latter by very much". He feels "considerable initial sympathy" with the objection from the quantity of suffering, which "seems to count against the claim that there is a God" [2].
- **Swinburne also accepts his theodicy (toward lower).** In the same chapter he gives his "final verdict": "a God would not be less than perfectly good if he were to bring about or allow to occur that amount of suffering that exists for the sake of the greater good that results". He adds that evil is not a good C-inductive argument against *Christian* theism, because afterlife and incarnation are already part of it [2]. So a leading specialist accepts a rival explanation as adequate.
- **Benton, Hawthorne and Isaacs (toward higher).** Writing in *Oxford Studies in Philosophy of Religion* (2016), they argue on standard Bayesian grounds that evil "is clearly evidence against the existence of God" and "can even constitute a lot of evidence", against skeptical theists who deny it [3]. I found no citable statement of the authors' own beliefs, so (b) does not rely on them.
- **The SEP entry defends the argument (toward higher).** Tooley's SEP entry (revised 2015) treats the evidential argument as live and defends it. It quotes Plantinga's judgement that most theodicies "strike me as tepid, shallow and ultimately frivolous", and argues that soul-making "provides no justification for the existence of any animal pain" [1]. Tooley defends the argument himself, so his verdict that "no theodicy… has been successful" is one side's view, not a report on the field.
- **Skeptical theism is contested within theism (both directions).** The SEP entry on skeptical theism (Perrine, 2023) presents it as a major family of replies, with an "extensive" literature of objections, and names Swinburne among its critics [4].
- **Davies on God's goodness (bears on f).** Brian Davies (2006) argues that the argument "misfires" because the God of classical theism is not a moral agent. Critics reply that this saves God's existence at the cost of a God "not good enough to care" [5]. Either way, the argument reaches a morally perfect God, not every God.
- **The datum itself is not disputed.** The depth of animal suffering before humans is standard palaeontology and evolutionary biology, and both Swinburne and Tooley build on it [1][2].

**Rule applied:** Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the quantity and spread of suffering, including animal suffering over deep time, is mainstream biology and is granted by both sides [1][2] (+1).
- (b) holds: Swinburne, named, grants that evil is evidence against God, writing P(h|e&k) < P(h|k) [2] (+1).
- (c) holds: the SEP entries on evil and on skeptical theism treat the evidential argument as the live core of the debate, not as refuted [1][4] (+1).
- (d) does not hold: no survey shows that Rowe's or Draper's premises are a minority view among specialists. Skeptical theism, the main denial, is itself contested by theists such as Swinburne [4].
- (e) holds: a rival explanation (greater goods, including free choice, a lawlike world, and an afterlife that compensates) is accepted as adequate by named specialists. Swinburne gives it as his "final verdict" [2], and the theodicy literature (van Inwagen, Hick, Stump, Murray) defends versions of it [1] (−1). Skeptical theism is not counted here, because it denies the datum is evidence without explaining it.
- (f) holds: the conclusion is "no omnipotent, perfectly good God". An indifferent creator (deism) or Davies's non-moral God is untouched, and Swinburne says the force falls on bare theism, not Christian theism [2][5] (−1). This is a different disagreement from (e): it is about what the argument rules out, not why suffering occurs.

Six.

**Proposed:** change 7→6. The single strongest reason is that the same leading theist who grants that evil counts against God also accepts a theodicy as adequate [2]. A theist concedes that suffering points away from God, while many accept an explanation of it. Confidence 60.

**Text fixes:**
- **status** (265 characters), to add the concession that now carries (b): "The most discussed argument against God. The logical version (evil and God cannot both exist) is widely considered answered; the evidential version is live, and theist Richard Swinburne grants that evil lowers the probability of God, though not by much in his view."
- **body, last paragraph.** "nearly every theist philosopher treats it as the serious objection" overstates, since Davies and other classical theists deny it gets a grip [5]. Replacement: "most theist philosophers of religion treat it as the serious objection, and some, like Swinburne, grant that it counts against God while denying that it counts for much."
- **sources:** add "Richard Swinburne, The Existence of God, 2nd ed. (2004), chapter 11" and "Michael Tooley, The Problem of Evil, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2015)".

**If the owner disagrees:** the fact to weigh is whether a field that is about 72% theist [26] counts as "many specialists" accepting the theodicy. If he discounts that field, (e) falls and the line stays at 7. If he also rules that "God" means only a perfectly good God, (f) falls too and it scores 8.

## against-02: If God wanted to be known, he could make it obvious

**Current:** strength 6, confidence 75. Status: "A major argument in philosophy of religion since the 1990s, taken seriously by theists who answer it with reasons God might stay hidden; not accepted as decisive by either side."

**Evidence:**
- **The SEP treats it as live (toward higher).** The SEP entry (Howard-Snyder and Green, revised 2022) says: "More than anyone else, Schellenberg has shaped the contemporary debate", tracing it to Schellenberg's *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason* (1993). Most critics accept that nonresistant nonbelief occurs and offer reasons God might permit it (Howard-Snyder, Swinburne, Hick, Murray, Moser, Dumsday). A minority deny it occurs (Jackson, Wainwright, and authors who appeal to the effects of sin on the mind) [6].
- **The relationship premise is attacked hardest (toward lower).** The most sustained attack is on the premise that a relationship with God cannot begin without belief. Critics offer non-doxastic faith and "thinner" belief states [6].
- **Swinburne grants it no weight (toward lower).** Swinburne treats honest agnosticism as the bad state "most evidently" taken as evidence against God, and calls the argument "a variant on the normal argument from evil". He gives a freedom-based explanation of it and concludes that "the argument from hiddenness does not constitute a good C-inductive argument against the existence of God": on his account it leaves the probability of theism unchanged [2]. Unlike evil, a leading theist grants it no weight and accepts a rival explanation.
- **Rea says it misses the Christian God (toward lower).** Rea (2018) holds that Schellenberg's argument "does not target the God of Christian theism" and treats hiddenness as "fundamentally a problem of violated expectations". His reviewer notes that "a problem lingers" [7].
- **Green finds Schellenberg overconfident (toward lower).** Adam Green's review of Schellenberg's *The Hiddenness Argument* (OUP 2015) calls Schellenberg's claim that the deductive argument beats all theistic evidence overconfident, and presses the move from culpable to nonresistant nonbelief [8].
- **Its scope is a perfectly loving God (bears on f).** Schellenberg says his argument targets theism, a personal and perfectly loving God, not religious truth in general: "I am not a religious disbeliever more generally" [9]. The SEP notes that an indifferent God, as in "some versions of Deism", blocks the premises [6].

**Rule applied:** Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the datum is that some people who are open to God do not believe. Most critics in the SEP survey grant it and argue about why God permits it [6] (+1).
- (b) does not hold: no named theist grants that hiddenness is evidence against God. Swinburne explicitly finds no net effect [2], Moser argues it "does not justify atheism", and Rea says it misses the Christian God [6][7].
- (c) holds: the SEP (2022) and the OUP literature treat it as a live, central argument [6][8] (+1).
- (d) does not hold: the premise that belief is needed to begin a relationship is heavily attacked [6], but no survey shows it is a minority view among specialists.
- (e) holds: named specialists accept rival explanations of nonresistant nonbelief as adequate. These are Swinburne's freedom and responsibility account, which he uses to conclude the argument fails [2], Dumsday's responsibility argument, and Murray's non-coercion and Hick's epistemic distance [6] (−1).
- (f) holds: the conclusion is "no perfectly loving God". Schellenberg himself leaves wider religious truth open [9], and an indifferent or deist creator is untouched [6] (−1). This is separate from (e): it is about scope, not about why nonbelief occurs.

Five.

**Proposed:** change 6→5. The single strongest reason is that the argument is live in every reference work, but no leading theist grants it weight, and a leading theist accepts an explanation of the datum [2][6]. Confidence 60.

**Text fixes:**
- **status** (264 characters), to date it accurately and state its scope: "A major argument in philosophy of religion since 1993, taken seriously by theists who answer it with reasons God might stay hidden or deny that belief is needed to begin a relationship; it targets a perfectly loving God and leaves an indifferent creator untouched."
- **body, theist-replies paragraph.** The strongest premise attack is missing. After "...resistance can be hidden even from oneself." insert: "Others deny that a relationship with God must begin with belief: trust or hope, they argue, can come first."
- **sources:** add "Daniel Howard-Snyder and Adam Green, Hiddenness of God, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2022)" and "J. L. Schellenberg, The Hiddenness Argument (2015)".

**If the owner disagrees:** the fact to weigh is the same as for against-01. Do freedom-based accounts accepted by theist philosophers of religion count as "accepted as adequate by many specialists"? If not, (e) falls and the line stays at 6. Against-01 must then be read the same way.

## against-06: Where God's action should be detectable, it is not

**Current:** strength 5, confidence 70. Status: "The large prayer trials found no aggregate effect; small earlier studies claiming benefits are disputed. Whether any miracle has been verified is disputed: skeptics find none confirmed, some scholars catalogue well-attested cases. Whether God would show up in tests is the contested premise."

**Evidence:**
- **STEP found no effect (toward higher).** STEP (Benson et al., 2006, about 1,800 bypass patients) found complications in 52% of prayed-for patients versus 51% of those not prayed for (RR 1.02). Patients told they were certain to be prayed for had 59% against 52% for those uncertain (RR 1.14) [10].
- **The pooled studies agree (confirms the datum).** A meta-analysis of 14 studies (Masters, Spielmans and Goodson, 2006) found g = .100, not different from zero, falling to .012 without one disputed study. The authors advised against funding more trials [11]. The Cochrane review (2009, ten studies, 7,646 patients) found "no clear effect" on death, judged the findings "equivocal", and was "not convinced that further trials… should be undertaken" [12].
- **The trial team disclaimed the inference (toward lower).** The Harvard Gazette reported that the STEP investigators "are quick to say that its results do not challenge the existence of God", and that the study did not try to address "whether God answers intercessory prayers" [14].
- **The SEP says such studies cannot test it (toward lower).** The SEP entry on petitionary prayer (Davison) calls such studies "flawed from the outset", citing Brümmer and Davison, because "God is typically assumed to be a free person, not a natural force that acts automatically in all similar cases" [13].
- **Lourdes cures are unexplained, not beyond nature (both directions).** Lourdes's International Medical Committee has listed 72 cures as unexplained "in the current state of scientific knowledge". The 72nd, announced in 2025, was a 2009 recovery from ALS [15]. Natural "ALS reversals" are documented, though: a 2018 study co-authored by Bedlack confirmed 36 cases [16]. "Unexplained" therefore does not mean "beyond nature".
- **The pro-miracle case is weak (toward lower, weakly).** Keener's two-volume *Miracles* (2011) catalogues contemporary healing reports and argues that dismissing them wholesale is a prior commitment [18]. The Brown et al. (2010) Mozambique study reported improved hearing and vision after "proximal" prayer, but it had 24 subjects and no control group, and has been criticised as methodologically weak [19].
- **Hume's argument does not carry the miracle half.** The body leans on Hume, but John Earman, a non-theist philosopher of science, judges Hume's in-principle argument an "abject failure" on Bayesian grounds (2000) [17]. The miracle half of the line rests on the record of investigations.

**Rule applied:** Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the key datum, that intercessory prayer has no detectable effect in controlled trials, is the mainstream reading in medical research [10][11][12] (+1).
- (b) does not hold: I found no named theist who grants that null prayer trials are evidence against God. The SEP's theist authorities say the studies cannot test it [13].
- (c) does not hold: no reference work treats "no detectable divine action, therefore no God" as a standing argument. The SEP prayer entry treats the tests as flawed [13], and the miracles half rests on Hume, whose in-principle argument Earman rejects [17].
- (d) holds: the argument needs the premise that a God who acts would show up in protocol trials or in investigations that satisfy skeptics. The trial team [14] and the SEP entry [13] reject it, and it is the line's own "contested premise". The SEP's view comes from theists, so this point carries the selection caveat in Rule problem 1 (−1).
- (e) is not charged separately: the theist account of the null trials (God is a free agent who does not answer test prayers on schedule) is only the denial of the (d) premise, so it is charged once.
- (f) holds: the conclusion is "no God who intervenes visibly". A non-intervening or deist God is untouched, as the body itself says, and this is the rule's own example of (f) (−1).

Four.

**Proposed:** change 5→4. The single strongest reason is that the datum is solid, but even the trial team says it does not reach God, and no reference work treats it as an argument against God [13][14]. Confidence 60.

**Text fixes:**
- **summary** (266 characters). Remove "no physical event has yet required a supernatural explanation", which is against-08's datum. Replacement: "Controlled studies of intercessory prayer find no effect, and no miracle claim has been confirmed to the satisfaction of skeptical investigators; a God who acts in the world in answer to prayer should leave traces where they are looked for, and none have been found."
- **status** (291 characters): "The large prayer trials and meta-analyses found no effect; small earlier positive studies are disputed. Lourdes lists 72 cures as unexplained; skeptics find none shown to exceed nature. Whether God would show up in tests is the contested premise, and a God who never intervenes is untouched."
- **best_reply** (323 characters). "have not held up" overstates the Lourdes record. Replacement: "Believers do claim that prayer heals and that miracles happen, and when such claims are checked by investigators without a stake in the outcome, none has been shown to exceed what natural processes can do; a God whose actions are indistinguishable from chance is, for every practical purpose, indistinguishable from no God."
- **body, paragraph 1.** Delete "In four centuries of science, no observation has required a supernatural cause.", which is against-08's datum. After the Lourdes sentence, add: "Lourdes's medical committee lists 72 cures as unexplained by current science; the latest, a recovery from ALS, is in a disease where natural reversals are also documented."
- **body, paragraph 4.** "Hume's point still holds: a mistaken or exaggerated report is always more likely than a suspension of nature." overstates. Replacement: "and that most reported miracles shrink under investigation, which lowers the weight of the rest. Hume went further and said no testimony could ever establish a miracle; many philosophers, including the non-theist John Earman, now judge that claim too strong."
- **sources:** add "Masters, Spielmans and Goodson, Are there demonstrable effects of distant intercessory prayer?, Annals of Behavioral Medicine (2006)" and "Roberts, Ahmed and Davison, Intercessory prayer for the alleviation of ill health, Cochrane (2009)".

## against-08: Natural explanations keep winning, and God keeps retreating

**Current:** strength 5, confidence 75. Status: "The historical pattern is real and theists mostly agree that \"God of the gaps\" reasoning is bad; they deny that the main arguments for God are gap arguments."

**Evidence:**
- **The SEP gives the historical argument (toward higher).** Papineau's SEP entry on naturalism (revised 2020) argues that naturalist doctrines "are closely responsive to received scientific opinion" and that "the acceptance of the causal closure of the physical led to full-fledged physicalism". It presents this as a main support for naturalism, contested in places [20].
- **Draper's Oxford Handbook chapter (toward higher).** Paul Draper (*Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Religion*, 2005) argues that the success of science in explaining natural phenomena naturally "strongly supports metaphysical naturalism over… theism". Critics answer with a "selectionist" reply: theories succeed because they survived selection, not because naturalism is true [21].
- **Historians reject the conflict narrative (toward lower).** Historians of science reject the "conflict" narrative the line leans on. Brooke calls it "largely discredited", and Harrison describes the field's consensus as a "complexity thesis" (Numbers, ed., *Galileo Goes to Jail*, 2009) [23].
- **The medical examples are overstated (toward lower).** Kroll and Bachrach (1984) studied 57 medieval accounts of madness, possession and epilepsy. Only 9 (16%) were tied to sin, and the sources name humours, diet, drink, overwork and grief as causes. They conclude that the stereotype of a general medieval belief that mental illness came from sin "is not supported by the evidence" [22].
- **Gap reasoning is defended, and theism expects natural causes (toward lower).** Robert Larmer (2002) argues that gap reasoning is not inherently fallacious and is dismissed too quickly [24]. Plantinga (2011) argues that theism expects a lawlike world explained by secondary causes, so natural explanations are no evidence against God [25].
- **The core pattern is accurate.** Newton's corrective God, defended in the Leibniz–Clarke exchange, gave way to Laplace's stability results, and Paley's design gave way to Darwin. No settled scientific explanation has been replaced by a divine one. Theists treat the Big Bang and fine-tuning as reversals of a different kind, and those are already scored as for-01 and for-02 [20][25].

**Rule applied:** Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the datum is that domain after domain once given a divine explanation now has a natural one, and none has gone the other way inside science. This is mainstream history and philosophy of science, even though the wider "conflict" framing is not [20][23] (+1).
- (b) does not hold: theists from Coulson to Plantinga grant that gap-filling theology is bad, but that concedes a point against a gap-filling God, not that the datum is evidence against God [25].
- (c) holds: the SEP entry on naturalism and Draper's Oxford Handbook chapter treat the argument from scientific success as live [20][21] (+1).
- (d) is charged once, under (e). The argument needs the premise that theism predicted gaps that would stay unclosed. Its denial is the theist secondary-causes account, so (d) and (e) are one disagreement.
- (e) holds: the rival account is that God ordinarily acts through natural causes, so theism expects natural explanations. The Augustine–Aquinas–Boyle tradition, Plantinga [25] and the historians' complexity thesis [23] accept it as adequate (−1).
- (f) holds: getting from "God is not needed inside science" to "no God" needs a further step. That step is that the questions science presupposes (why there are laws, why anything exists) are gaps of the same kind. It is contested by naturalists and theists alike, and it is a separate disagreement from (e) (−1).

Five.

**Proposed:** keep 5. The single strongest reason is that the historical datum is real and argued in reference works, but a theism without gaps absorbs it, and the last step to "no God" is open [21][25]. Confidence 65.

**Text fixes:**
- **body, paragraph 1.** "Disease was widely read as divine judgment before germ theory, and mental illness as possession before psychiatry." is overstated [22]. Replacement: "Epidemics were often preached as divine punishment before germ theory, alongside the physicians' own natural theories."
- **body, paragraph 1.** "and nothing has gone the other way" is contested by theists who cite the Big Bang. Replacement: "and no settled natural explanation has been replaced by a divine one."
- **best_reply** (337 characters). "the questions now called 'beyond science' are the ones science has not yet reached" asserts the contested (f) step as fact. Replacement: "Every past retreat was defended in the same words at the time, and some questions once called beyond science, such as the fit of living things to their world, turned out not to be; the induction from four centuries is that waiting has worked better than invoking, though whether it reaches why there are laws at all is the open question."
- **sources:** add "Paul Draper, God, Science, and Naturalism, in The Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Religion (2005)" and "David Papineau, Naturalism, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (2020)".

**If the owner disagrees:** the call is whether the secondary-causes reply (e) and the "deeper questions are not gaps" step (f) are one disagreement or two. If one, the line scores 6. Against-06 was scored on the matching reading: one disagreement about whether the datum is expected, one about scope.

## Rule problems (not scored)

1. **The specialist base for (e) is lopsided, and (e) is the rule's biggest lever on my lines.** Against-01 and against-02 each lose a point under (e) because named theist philosophers of religion accept a theodicy as adequate.
   - In the 2009 PhilPapers survey, 72.3% of philosophy-of-religion specialists accepted or leaned toward theism, against 72.8% atheism among all respondents [26]. Draper and Nichols (*Monist*, 2013) diagnose partisanship and group bias in the field [27].
   - In a field with that skew, "many specialists accept the rival explanation" will hold for almost any theist reply, so (e) tilts against the against-column. The same skew affects (b) in the other direction, and (d).
   - A stricter reading would count an (e) explanation only if specialists who reject the line's conclusion also judge it adequate, or a reference work reports it as widely accepted. That would restore against-01 to 7 and against-02 to 6, leave 06 and 08 unchanged, and give an against sum of 41 and a headline of 42/83 = 50.6%. I did not apply it, because it is a rule change and the for-groups will likely apply (e) on its plain text (visions for for-08, the multiverse for for-02, physicalism for for-05).
   - Suggested rewording for (d) and (e): "...among specialists in its field. Where the field is lopsided on the conclusion itself, the view must also be held by specialists who reject the line's conclusion."
2. **Explanation versus defense.** The rule does not say whether a reply that denies the datum's force without explaining it counts as a "rival explanation" under (e). Examples are skeptical theism, Davies's non-moral God, and "it's a brute fact". I read it as not counting. The same reading must then be applied to brute-fact replies against for-03 and for-06. Suggested addition to (e): "A reply that only denies the datum is evidence, without saying why it occurs, is not a rival explanation."
3. **(d), (e) and (f) can charge one disagreement two or three times.** In against-06, "God would answer test prayers" (d) and "God does not answer test prayers" (e) are one proposition. In against-08, "theism predicts gaps" (d) and "theism predicts natural causes" (e) are the same. Suggested addition: "A single disagreement is charged once, under whichever of (d), (e) or (f) fits best."
4. **What "God" means for (f).** The for-column example is "a cause → a personal God"; the against-column example is "no detectable action → no God". The rule does not say whether a deist or non-moral creator counts as God, and against-01 and against-02 each move by one point on that alone. Suggested addition: "'God' means a personal creator; an argument that rules out only a perfectly good, perfectly loving or intervening God takes (f)."
5. **(b) needs a checkable threshold.** A theist who grants that a datum is evidence against God is rare by selection, and Swinburne is unusual in stating likelihoods. Suggested wording: "a named leading defender writes that the datum lowers the probability of their side, even slightly." Swinburne's verdict on evil, P(h|e&k) < P(h|k), qualifies. His verdict on hiddenness, that it leaves the probability unchanged, does not.
6. **The site note contradicts the headline.** site.json `evidence_note` says "The two columns are not meant to be added up", but the home-page headline adds them. This is not a rule problem for my lines, but the owner should know the published note contradicts the arithmetic.
