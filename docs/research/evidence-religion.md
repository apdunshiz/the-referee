**What the research changes and what it confirms.**
- **for-07 rises by one.** Both Stanford Encyclopedia entries on the topic (Religious Experience, 2022; Mysticism, 2026) treat the argument from experience as live. The Mysticism entry concludes that the defenders' premises "yield prima facie evidence in favor of the validity of (some) experiences of God".
- **for-08 rises by one.** Its datum, that the first followers sincerely believed they saw Jesus, is close to universal among historians. The one reference work on miracle inference (SEP Miracles, 2024) describes the historian's refusal to infer miracles as one side of "a deep conceptual fault line", not as the majority view. - **against-03 is confirmed at 5, with text fixes.** Its text leans on "hyperactive agency detection", the weakest-supported part of the cognitive science of religion: controlled studies have not linked agency detection to religious belief (Maij et al. 2019; Van Leeuwen and van Elk 2019). Two tests were fixed and applied the same way on all four lines:
- (b) needs an unconditional concession by a named leading defender of the other side.
- (d) holds only where a survey or reference work says the needed premise is a minority view.

The owner may prefer different tests. Changing either moves for-08 and against-04 first.

## for-07: People across every culture report experiences of the divine

**Current:** strength 4, confidence 70. Status: "The reports are well documented; whether they are evidence for anything beyond the human mind is disputed, and the sheer variety of what is reported is a known difficulty."

**Specialists** for this line are philosophers of religion for the inference, and psychologists and anthropologists of religion for the datum and its rival explanations.

**Evidence:**
- *Toward higher.* The SEP Religious Experience entry (Webb, 2022) presents Swinburne's principle of credulity and Alston's doxastic-practice argument as live positions, not refuted ones. It says "Anyone who accepts the principle has excellent reason to accept the deliverances of religious experience" unless defeaters always apply. It also says "it is not clear whether the differences are great enough to disqualify religious testimony always and everywhere" [1].
- *Toward higher.* The SEP Mysticism entry (rev. 2026) sets out the perceptual model defended by Swinburne, Wainwright and Gellman and concludes that its premises "yield prima facie evidence in favor of the validity of (some) experiences of God". It rejects the pathology model, noting that ecstatic seizure experiences are "very rare" [2].
- *Toward higher.* Webb's entry says "To argue that the experience is illusory because there is a corresponding brain state is fallacious" [1]. Neural correlates alone do not defeat the argument.
- *Toward lower.* The same Mysticism entry calls conflicting claims a serious problem: "This conflict of claims undercuts the reliability of mystical claims as a source of knowledge." It reports Gale, Fales, C. B. Martin and Rowe's objection that experiences of God cannot be cross-checked, so "we have no way of determining when an experience of God is delusory" [2].
- *Toward lower.* The Mysticism entry grants that naturalistic explanations offer "a reasonable alternative explanation". It reports Bagger's argument that "Invoking God to explain mystical experiences is like invoking miracles to explain natural phenomena" [2].
- *Toward lower.* In a double-blind trial, high-dose psilocybin given to religiously active volunteers occasioned experiences "similar to spontaneously occurring mystical experiences". Two months later, volunteers rated these experiences as having substantial personal meaning and spiritual significance (Griffiths et al. 2006) [3].
- *Toward lower.* Mackie's reply to Swinburne is probabilistic: ordinary error or misinterpretation is more probable than a supernatural cause, so the seeming should not be trusted (summarised in [4]). No named non-theist critic in either SEP entry grants the experiences prima facie force [1][2].

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the key datum, that experiences of a presence or the sacred are reported across cultures and often change lives, is the mainstream view in the psychology and anthropology of religion. It is undisputed in both SEP entries [1][2] and reproducible in the laboratory [3] (+1).
- (b) does not hold: neither SEP entry names a leading non-theist who grants the reports prima facie evidential force. Mackie, Gale, Fales, Rowe and Bagger all deny it [2][4].
- (c) holds: both SEP entries treat the argument as live and contested, not refuted [1][2] (+1).
- (d) does not hold, though this is uncertain. The needed premise is that seemings confer prima facie justification (credulity, or phenomenal conservatism). No survey of philosophers of religion or epistemologists measures this, and the SEP entries present it as a standing position, not a fringe one. See Rule problems.
- (e) holds: naturalistic explanations (cultural expectation, neurophysiology, drugs) are accepted as adequate by many specialists. Even the sympathetic Mysticism entry calls them "a reasonable alternative explanation" [2] (−1).
- (f) holds: the experiences range from a personal creator to an impersonal absolute to "nothingness" [1]. Getting from "something is there" to God needs a further contested step (−1).
- Five.

**Proposed:** change 4→5. The single strongest reason is that both standard reference entries treat the argument as live and say it gives at least prima facie evidence, and the rule then lands at 5. The line's datum is at least as solid as for-04's or for-05's, which sit at 5. Confidence 55, because (d) cannot be checked against survey data and the (e) penalty overlaps against-03 (see Double counting).

**Text fixes:**
- best_objection relies on "stimulation of the temporal lobe". The line's own body says that result "failed a double-blind replication", and the best objection should not rest on a failed result. Replace with (302 characters): "The experiences disagree with one another along religious lines, which is what you would expect if they are produced by culture and brain rather than by contact with one reality; and in a double-blind trial psilocybin produced experiences that volunteers rated as having lasting spiritual significance."
- Body, last paragraph: "This counts for a little" would understate a 5. If the change is made, use "This counts for something, more if you have had such an experience yourself, ...". Leave the rest of the sentence as it is.
- No other false sentences found. The best_reply's "shared core" claim is the perennialist position, and constructivists dispute it, but as a stated reply it is fair.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to weigh the conflicting-content objection as a near-defeater. That means treating the SEP Mysticism line "This conflict of claims undercuts the reliability of mystical claims" as outweighing the same entry's conclusion of prima facie evidence. If he does, the honest place to score it is under (d) or (e), which the rule has already charged once.

## for-08: The earliest followers of Jesus sincerely believed they saw him alive after his death

**Current:** strength 5, confidence 75. Status: "The crucifixion, the early appearance claims and Paul's conversion are accepted by most historians of every persuasion, and James's earlier unbelief by many; what caused the appearance beliefs is where historians divide, with most non-Christian scholars favouring visions or grief experiences."

**Specialists** for this line are historians of early Christianity and New Testament scholars, believers and non-believers. No random-sample survey of them exists. The only literature count is Habermas's, an apologist's unofficial and non-random tally, labelled as such below.

**Evidence:**
- *Toward higher, datum.* Habermas's count of about 1,400 German, French and English publications since 1975 reports "the nearly unanimous consent" of critical scholars that the early followers "thought that they had seen the risen Jesus". About 75% favour arguments for the empty tomb. He calls his figures "a rough estimate" and "unofficial", and says they prove nothing about whether the resurrection occurred [7]. This is an apologist's survey with a non-random sample.
- *Toward higher, datum.* On the dating of the 1 Cor 15:3–7 tradition, Lüdemann, a sceptic, dates it "within the first two years after the crucifixion". Funk and the Jesus Seminar allow "two or three years at most", Koester "no later than within five years", and Dunn "within months". Ehrman questions how precise such dating can be, and Marxsen calls the formula "relatively late" [12].
- *Toward higher, reference works.* The SEP Miracles entry (McGrew and Larmer, 2024) treats the minimal-facts argument as live and contested, not refuted. It calls a blanket "historians can never establish a miracle" argument "crude". It also notes that Hume's critique "is now viewed in some quarters as requiring defense" [10]. McGrew is himself a defender of the argument.
- *Toward lower, premise.* Ehrman, in the 2006 debate, said "by definition a miracle is the least probable occurrence", "Historians can only establish what probably happened in the past", and "I wish we could establish miracles, but we can't". He grants the crucifixion and Paul's claim of a vision, but says the burial and empty tomb "could be a later invention" [8].
- *Toward lower, rival explanation.* Ehrman argues that "the disciples' belief in the resurrection was based on visionary experiences" of a few (Peter, Paul, Mary, probably James) and spread by testimony [9]. Lüdemann argues for a guilt-driven vision by Peter [9].
- *Both directions.* Allison, a critical scholar who is a believer, leans toward an empty tomb and real experiences. He judges the tomb "was probably found empty" and "we can be fairly sure that several people did ostensibly see Jesus after his death". He also judges it "harder than most apologists would admit to dismiss skeptical explanations", and that neither side can prove its case [11].
- *Toward higher, rival explanation's standing.* Schröter and Jacobi's *Jesus Handbook* (2022) say the view that the appearances were subjective and the tomb not empty "remains a minority" in New Testament scholarship (as reported in [13]).

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: the key datum, that the first followers sincerely believed Jesus appeared to them, is accepted across the field, by Ehrman and Lüdemann as well as Habermas [7][8][9] (+1).
- (b) does not hold: the leading non-Christian historians grant the belief but not that it is evidence for a resurrection. Ehrman explicitly denies the historian can infer one [8]. No leading non-theist specialist found grants it as some evidence for God.
- (c) holds: the SEP Miracles entry treats the historical argument as live [10] (+1).
- (d) does not hold. The argument needs the premise that a historian may rank "God raised Jesus" as the best explanation. Ehrman rejects it on method [8], but the one reference work on the question does not call the rejection the majority view. The SEP Miracles entry says Humean positions are endorsed in "many" historical-Jesus studies and describes them as "one side of a deep conceptual fault line" in biblical studies [10]. A divided field is not a minority premise. The same test is used on all four lines: (d) holds only where a survey or reference work says the premise is a minority view.
- (e) holds: the vision or bereavement explanation is accepted as adequate by many specialists. Ehrman and Lüdemann defend it [9], and Habermas himself counts "more than forty scholars" proposing naturalistic alternatives [7]. It is a minority overall [13], but the rule asks for "many", not "most" (−1).
- (f) does not hold for this column. The step from "a resurrection happened" to "God exists" is not seriously contested. The further step to the Christian God matters for the worldview scores, not the God/no-God headline.
- Six.

**Proposed:** change 5→6. The single strongest reason is that the datum is near-certain, and the miracle-inference premise is contested in a divided field rather than a minority view. Only the rival vision explanation costs a point. Confidence 45. This was 5 under an earlier draft of this report, which charged (d) on Ehrman's word alone. That was uneven with how (d) was judged for for-07 and against-04.

**Text fixes:**
- The summary states James's prior unbelief as fact ("a brother who had not followed him joined them"), while the status says only "many" historians accept it. Replace the summary with (290 characters): "Jesus was crucified; within a few years his followers were proclaiming that he had appeared to them, and a persecutor joined them, as, on many historians' reading, did a brother who had not followed him; if a resurrection happened, it is strong evidence for the Christian God in particular."
- Body: "within a few years a creed was circulating" is fair for the majority dating [12]. Optionally add ", on most scholars' dating," after "within a few years", since Ehrman and Marxsen dissent.
- The best_objection's "every other religion's founding miracles are rejected on exactly this kind of evidence" is against-04's symmetry argument. It is fine as an objection, since objections are not scored, but see Double counting.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to treat the historian's refusal to infer miracles, Ehrman's principle [8], as the field's settled practice, not one side of the "deep conceptual fault line" the SEP describes [10]. If he does, (d) holds and the line stays at 5.

## against-03: Religion has natural explanations that work whether or not God exists

**Current:** strength 5, confidence 70. Status: "The cognitive and social accounts are well supported; whether they debunk religious belief or merely describe the machinery God might have used is a philosophical dispute."

**Specialists** for this line are cognitive scientists of religion for the datum (a) and its rivals (e). The debunking premise (d) falls to epistemologists and philosophers of religion.

**Evidence:**
- *Toward higher.* The SEP's new entry Evolutionary Approaches to Religion (2026) treats debunking arguments as live: "it is doubtful that debunking arguments will go away anytime soon". It reports that theist replies have problems. The metaphysical reply "arguably misconstrues the fundamentally epistemological nature of the challenge", and the entry adds that origins can still raise an epistemic problem even though they do not show falsity [14].
- *Toward higher.* Jong and Visala (2014) argue that debunking arguments which show a belief-forming mechanism to be falsehood-tracking or deceptive do succeed against theism. They deny that cognitive science of religion has shown this [17].
- *Toward higher.* Thurow (2023) denies full debunking but grants that, if cognitive science predicts that believers rationalise their beliefs, awareness of that could produce partial defeat or require lower confidence (as reported in [14]).
- *Toward lower, datum.* The same SEP entry calls cognitive science of religion "still a relatively new research program and not everyone is on board". It calls agency detection "controversial", says it is "unlikely to give a complete account of religious belief", and notes that some priming results have not replicated [14].
- *Toward lower, datum.* Maij, van Schie and van Elk (2019) found that threat cues did not increase false agent detection. Participants were biased toward assuming agents were absent, and agency measures were unrelated to supernatural belief [15]. Van Leeuwen and van Elk (2019) conclude that general religious beliefs are mostly learned from surrounding culture. Their model "inverts" the agency-detection theory: belief drives people to seek agency experiences, not the reverse [16].
- *Toward lower, inference.* Clark and Barrett (2011) argue that the findings of cognitive science of religion fit a Reidian epistemology on which naturally arising beliefs are justified unless defeated, and may even support a god-faculty [18]. The SEP reports that the sensitivity condition many debunking arguments use is "widely rejected" in epistemology because it has sceptical implications [14]. Launonen (2021) argues that debunking arguments "gain little" from cognitive science of religion [20].

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: that religious belief arises from ordinary cognitive biases plus cultural learning is the mainstream framework in cognitive science of religion and the social study of religion [14][16], even though the agency-detection version of it is weakly supported [15] (+1).
- (b) does not hold. The test used on all four lines is an unconditional concession by a named leading defender of the other side. The leading theist respondents (Barrett, Clark, Murray, Plantinga) treat the findings as neutral or even friendly [14][18]. Jong and Visala concede only that a debunking argument *would* succeed if a mechanism were shown to track falsehood [17]. Thurow grants partial defeat only *if* cognitive science predicts rationalisation [14]. Both concessions are conditional, so neither counts.
- (c) holds: the SEP treats debunking arguments as live and unresolved [14] (+1).
- (d) holds: the argument needs a debunking premise, that an explanation of how a belief arises with no truth-link defeats it, usually via a sensitivity condition. The SEP reports that condition is "widely rejected" among epistemologists [14] (−1).
- (e) does not hold: the datum is the existence of natural cognitive and social explanations. The only rival account of that datum, a designed sensus divinitatis [18], is not accepted as adequate by many cognitive scientists.
- (f) holds: from "belief is naturally explained" to "no God" is a further contested step. The line's own body says "this does not show God does not exist" (−1).
- Five.

**Proposed:** keep 5. The datum is mainstream and the debunking challenge is live in the reference literature, but the debunking premise is minority epistemology and the step to "no God" is one the line itself disclaims. Confidence 60.

**Text fixes:** The number holds, but four passages overstate agency detection, which the newest empirical work does not support [15][16].
- Summary: change only the opening clause, "Cognitive science shows that human minds are primed to see agents and purposes everywhere", to "Cognitive science finds that human minds readily read agency and purpose into events". Keep the rest as it is.
- Status (249 characters): "The social accounts are well supported; the cognitive ones are an active research programme, with agency detection its weakest-supported part; whether they debunk religious belief or describe machinery God might have used is a philosophical dispute."
- best_reply. The current "agent-detection that produces gods fires on wind, shadows and bad luck and is never corrected" rests on the weak agency-detection hypothesis. Replace with (231 characters): "Beliefs about other minds are checked by feedback all day, while intuitions of agency and purpose behind events are rarely open to correction; mechanisms that yield thousands of incompatible gods are not a reliable detector of one."
- Body, first sentence: replace "Human minds come fitted with a hair-trigger for detecting agents, a habit of reading purpose into events, and a readiness to accept what trusted elders say. These traits are useful: it is cheaper to mistake a branch for a snake than the reverse." with "Human minds readily read agency and purpose into events and accept what trusted elders say. One early theory held that we evolved a hair-trigger for detecting agents, because it is cheaper to mistake a branch for a snake than the reverse; controlled studies have not borne that part out."
- Body, reply paragraph: "the same machinery that produces one God produces Zeus, ancestor spirits and the evil eye" stays true.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to treat the debunking premise as mainstream epistemology, not minority. That means setting aside the SEP's report that the sensitivity condition is "widely rejected", which would make it 6.

## against-04: The world's religions contradict each other, and belief follows birthplace

**Current:** strength 5, confidence 80. Status: "The facts are undisputed; theists answer that one revelation can be true while others are partial or mistaken, and that the geography of belief is a fact about people, not about God."

**Specialists** for this line are philosophers of religion and epistemologists working on religious diversity and disagreement. The sociology of religion supplies the transmission datum.

**Evidence:**
- *Toward higher.* The SEP Religious Diversity (Pluralism) entry (Basinger, 2025) reports that Alston, a leading theist epistemologist, grants that "the reality of religious diversity diminishes justification", while holding that exclusivism stays rational [21].
- *Toward higher.* Plantinga, in *Warranted Christian Belief* (OUP 2000), concedes "knowledge of others who think differently can reduce one's degree of belief in Christian teaching". He also concedes that it "can also deprive the believer of the knowledge" and "could initially serve as a defeater". His conclusion is still that pluralism "need not constitute a defeater" [22].
- *Toward higher.* Kitcher (*Life After Faith*, 2014) argues that "the bases of belief are remarkably similar across the entire array of religious traditions", mostly teaching absorbed in childhood, so "complete symmetry prevails" (quoted in [23]). De Cruz (*Religious Disagreement*, Cambridge Elements 2019) treats others' religious beliefs as significant higher-order evidence, while advising against automatic agnosticism [25].
- *Toward lower.* Plantinga's reply to Kitcher: "To suppose that complete symmetry prevails ... is already to reject, e.g., Christian or even theistic belief", so the symmetry "can't sensibly be proposed as part of an argument against religious belief" [23].
- *Toward lower.* Feldman, who argues against Plantinga that knowing a rival's evidence is as good as yours makes retaining belief unreasonable, still judges Plantinga's replies to the moral charges "entirely satisfactory" and many epistemic charges "baseless" [24].
- *Toward lower.* The SEP entry presents exclusivist, inclusivist and Hick-style pluralist responses as standing options. It also reports criticisms of Hick: Clark argues secular commitments transform lives too, and Heim calls unitary pluralists "inclusivists in disguise" [21].
- *Scope.* The line's own body says it is "the strongest argument against any particular religion, more than against God in general". That is the (f) step below.

**Rule applied:**
- Start at 5.
- (a) holds: that religions make incompatible claims and that religion is mostly transmitted by family and culture is uncontested in the sociology and philosophy of religion [21][23] (+1).
- (b) holds, on Alston alone. The same test as against-03 applies: an unconditional concession by a named leading defender. Alston grants without condition that "the reality of religious diversity diminishes justification" [21] (+1). Plantinga's concession is conditional ("possible, though not necessary"; pluralism "need not constitute a defeater") [22], so it supports the point but does not carry it. This is still marginal, because Alston's concession concerns Christian practice, not theism as such. See Rule problems.
- (c) holds: the SEP treats the epistemic challenge of diversity as live [21] (+1).
- (d) does not hold, though this is uncertain. The needed premise is a symmetry or conciliationist principle, and no survey measures it directly. The 2020 PhilPapers survey asked about peer disagreement, but its breakdown could not be retrieved for this report. See Rule problems.
- (e) holds: the rival account of the datum, that one revelation is true and others partial or mistaken, with belief formed by upbringing as all belief is, is accepted as adequate by many philosophers of religion (Plantinga, Alston) [21][22] (−1).
- (f) holds: from "at most one religion is right" to "no God" is a further contested step, which the line's own body concedes (−1).
- Six.

**Proposed:** change 5→6. The single strongest reason is that Alston, a leading defender of the rationality of exclusivist belief, concedes without condition that diversity diminishes justification, and Plantinga concedes it can. Confidence 50, because (b) is marginal: the concession targets Christian belief, and the line's force against God in general is weaker than against any one religion.

**Text fixes:**
- best_reply ends "...is the same problem as hiddenness in another form". That borrows against-02's datum and invites counting it twice. Replace with (223 characters): "Scientific disagreements get settled by shared evidence and converge over time, while religious ones have not, and each tradition's support is the same kind its rivals offer: scripture, miracle reports and inner conviction."
- Body: "predicted by their parents and their map coordinates far better than by any argument" is unmeasured. Replace with "predicted largely by their parents and their map coordinates".
- Sources: the Pew report is used for the distribution of belief, which is the datum, not as evidence that a belief is true, so it does not breach the "no numbers of believers" rule. Say so if challenged. A transmission study would be a cleaner citation than a headcount.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to decide whether Alston's concession that diversity lowers justification for *Christian* belief counts as conceding evidence against *God*. If not, (b) fails and the line stays at 5.

## Double counting

**for-07 vs against-03 (required pair): distinct data, overlapping use.**
- for-07's datum is first-person experiences of a presence or the sacred. against-03's datum is the cognitive biases and social processes that produce religious *belief*. The newest model says general belief is mostly cultural, with experience feeding only personal belief [16].
- The overlap is in the scoring. Under the draft rule, for-07 loses 1 under (e) because naturalistic explanations are an adequate rival, and against-03 gains its whole value from roughly the same family of explanations. The datum is not counted twice, but the rival explanation is (see Rule problems).
- Merge options:
  - Fold for-07 into against-03 as one "against" line ("Religious belief and experience have natural explanations") at 5. Today that gives 38/80 = 47.5%; with the proposals, 39/82 = 47.6%.
  - Fold against-03 into for-07 at 5. Today that gives 42/79 = 53.2%; with the proposals, 44/82 = 53.7%.
- Either merge moves the headline about 3 points, for structural reasons only: it removes a line from one column and so changes the ratio. Recommendation: keep both, since the data are distinct.

**for-07 vs against-04: distinct.**
- Experiences (for-07) and contradictory doctrines and birthplace (against-04) are different data.
- The variety of experiences is used once against for-07, as the reason (f) holds. The diversity of doctrine is against-04's own datum. Those are related but not identical, so no merge is needed.
- Keep for-07's best_objection on variety. Objections are not scored.

**against-03 vs against-04: partial overlap.**
- against-03's social half ("religions arising, spreading and dying by ordinary social processes") and against-04's "belief follows birthplace" are the same fact, that religion is socially transmitted.
- Fix in text rather than by merging. Either trim against-03 to the cognitive half, or drop "belief follows birthplace" from against-04 and keep only the contradiction. A text-only fix changes no score.
- A full merge of against-03 into against-04 at 6 would give 42/79 = 53.2% today, or 44/82 = 53.7% with the proposals. That is a structural shift from removing a line.

**against-04 vs against-02 (hiddenness): distinct data.**
- Hiddenness rests on nonresistant nonbelief. Diversity rests on conflicting belief.
- against-04's best_reply borrows hiddenness, and the text fix above removes that.

**for-08 vs against-04:** for-08's best_objection uses against-04's symmetry argument. Objections are not scored, so there is no double count.

The other required pairs (for-02/against-05, for-05/against-07, for-01/for-03, against-02/06/08) do not touch these four lines and are left to the groups that own them.

## Rule problems (not scored)

1. **A rival explanation can be counted twice.** When a rival explanation of a "for" datum is itself an "against" line, (e) charges it once on the "for" line and the "against" line scores it again. for-07 loses 1 for naturalistic explanations, which is against-03's content. Suggested wording for (e): "Subtract 1 if a rival explanation of the datum is accepted as adequate by many specialists, unless that rival is scored as its own line on the other side; then note the link instead." Applied to for-07, that would give 6. The owner should decide which double count he prefers.
2. **(d) cannot be checked where no survey exists.** No survey measures whether philosophers accept the principle of credulity (for-07), whether NT historians accept miracle inference (for-08), or conciliationism (against-04). Each (d) ruling here is a judgement from reference works. Suggested wording: "(d) holds only where a survey or a standard reference work states the premise is a minority view; otherwise it does not hold, and the line's confidence drops 10."
3. **(b) is ambiguous for lines aimed at a particular religion.** Plantinga and Alston concede that diversity lowers justification for *Christian* belief, not for theism. for-08 has the mirror problem: it is evidence for one religion. Suggested wording: "(b) counts concessions about the line's own conclusion as worded; where that conclusion is narrower than God or no God, (f) applies."
4. **"Specialists" is two groups for some lines.** For for-07 and against-03, the datum belongs to psychologists and cognitive scientists, and the inference belongs to philosophers. Suggested wording: "(a) and (e) are judged by the specialists in the datum; (d) by the specialists in the needed premise."
5. **Who counts as "the other side" for (b).** Critical scholars who are believers, such as Allison and Jong, sit between the sides. Suggested wording: "Count a concession under (b) only from someone whose published view rejects the line's conclusion."
