## Summary

Current total 26 (history 7, consistency 5, explanation 7, testability 1, moral 6). Proposed total 24 (history 6, consistency 5, explanation 7, testability 1, moral 5).

The research confirms both history plus-ones (an early, stable text; a continuously documented community) and the explanation 7: all four answers are held across the astika schools, not one, and the karma bonus has better cross-divide support than the file claims (Weber, Herman, Reichenbach and Kaufman all treat karma as a serious systematic theodicy, whatever they conclude). It changes two lines. History loses a point because no deduction was applied for foundational claims that conflict with the record, while Judaism and Islam each lost one for the same kind of fact: the tradition's own dating of the Mahabharata war and the Kali Yuga to 3102 BCE, and the Indus-Vedic continuity claim, run against archaeology, linguistics and ancient DNA. Moral loses a point because the second class, persecution or legal coercion of dissenters or apostates, was never applied, although Nepal's constitutionally Hindu kingdom jailed people for converting others and Hindu-nationalist anti-conversion laws cover about ten Indian states; Islam was debited for exactly this class. Consistency and testability stand.

## History

**Current:** 7, confidence 55. "Start at 5. It has no founder and no founding event. Foundations that hold: the Vedas are among the oldest religious texts (plus 1) and the tradition is a continuous, documented community for three millennia (plus 1). Its stories of gods and ages are treated as myth in much of its own tradition and as history in some, which is not a failed claim; modern claims of a Vedic golden age of science are popular claims and are not scored. Seven."

**Evidence:**
- The Rigveda's latest hymns date to about 1200-1000 BCE, with composition over several centuries before that; transmission was oral, and the padapatha and related techniques preserved the text with a fidelity Staal calls the most impressive evidence for oral memory anywhere (Jamison and Brereton 2014 [4]; Staal 1986 [5]). The plus-one holds. Caveat: the Vedic religion of the hymns is ancestral to, not identical with, the Hinduism scored here.
- Continuous community: Lorenzen argues against the "colonial invention" school that a self-conscious Hindu identity formed between about 1200 and 1500 CE in rivalry with Islam, and that the practices it names run back without break through the Puranic and Brahmanical periods (Lorenzen 1999 [6]; Michaels 2004 [7]). The plus-one holds on the same standard that gave Judaism its 2,500-year point, though "three millennia" overstates the continuity of a single community.
- Against the record, case 1, epic and Puranic chronology. The tradition's own calendar starts the Kali Yuga on 18 February 3102 BCE, with the Mahabharata war just before it; the genre name for the epics is itihasa, "thus it happened." Excavation at Hastinapura found the earliest occupation in Painted Grey Ware levels of about 1100-800 BCE, with no urban settlement in the Ganga-Yamuna doab anywhere near 3100 BCE (Lal 1954-55 and 2013 [9]). The excavator himself placed the war around 900 BCE. In 2007 the Archaeological Survey of India told the Supreme Court there was no historical or scientific evidence for the Ramayana's characters or for Ram Setu as a man-made structure; the government withdrew it under political pressure and suspended two officials, which shows the tradition's representatives treat Rama's historicity as a foundation claim (DNA India 2007 [10]).
- Against the record, case 2, Indus-Vedic continuity and indigenous Aryans. Witzel's survey of the textual evidence concludes the Rigveda's horizon is the Panjab, it knows copper but not iron, and it was composed by the Puru and Bharata poets rather than by a population long resident in India; the horse-and-chariot culture of the hymns is absent from the Indus cities (Witzel 2001 [2]). Ancient DNA from over 500 individuals shows Steppe ancestry reaching South Asia in the first half of the second millennium BCE, the "likely conduit" for Indo-European languages (Narasimhan et al. 2019 [1]). The Ghaggar-Hakra, identified by continuity advocates with the Vedic Sarasvati as a glacier-fed river, was monsoon-fed and dried as the monsoon weakened (Giosan et al. 2012 [8]). Bryant's survey gives the indigenous-Aryan case a hearing without finding it has overturned the consensus (Bryant 2001 [3]).
- For the current score: Doniger and Flood both treat the epics as literature rather than failed history, and the miracle content (Krishna lifting Govardhan, the monkey army) is outside history's reach by the rule. The datable, non-miraculous claims are what conflict.

**Rule applied:** Start at 5. Holds: early, stable text (+1); continuous, documented community (+1). Gone against: claims that conflict with the record, the itihasa chronology and the Indus-Vedic continuity claim, one kind (−1). No founder, so late biography neither counts nor costs. Six.

**Proposed:** change 7→6. Strongest reason: the rule was applied to Judaism (Exodus and conquest, −1) and Islam (accounts of earlier prophets, −1) for claims their own liberal wings also treat as myth; the file's defence, "treated as myth in much of its own tradition," would restore those points too. Confidence 60.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to hold that a Kali Yuga date printed in every panchanga and a war the tradition's own genre calls history are not foundational claims, while the Exodus is.

## Consistency

**Current:** 5, confidence 50. "Start at 8. Less a system than a family of systems, from strict monism to personal theism to near-atheism; three core matters need careful definition before Hinduism can be held to any one account: whether there is a personal God, whether the self is real, and how karma relates to grace. Five."

**Evidence:**
- Personal God: Ramanuja's Sri Bhashya argues Advaita's Brahman, a substance without properties, is incoherent, and his seven objections (saptavidha-anupapatti) hold that avidya has no possible locus, since the jiva that is supposed to host it is itself a product of avidya (SEP "Concepts of God" [18]; Sri Bhashya debate [19]). Judged on the tradition's own best statements, the dispute is internal and unresolved after nine centuries.
- The self: Śaṅkara holds the self is pure non-intentional consciousness identical with Brahman (SEP "Śaṅkara" [17]); Nyaya and Samkhya hold the self is a real, plural substance. Both are "Hindu."
- Karma and freedom: a 2017 survey in Logica Universalis finds the tradition's own statements range from determinism to libertarian free will and shows the two cannot be held together without restricting karma's scope (Logica Universalis 2017 [20]). The Srivaishnava split over whether grace needs any human act (Vadakalai versus Tenkalai) is the same tension in devotional form.
- For a higher score: each school taken alone is as tight as any system on the list; Advaita arguably tighter. But the site scores Hinduism as one worldview, and Christianity was debited three points for doctrines that likewise split its tradition.
- For a lower score: Nyaya's created-but-not-from-nothing cosmos and Advaita's acosmism are two cosmologies, not two readings of one doctrine (Nyayakusumanjali [21]); a fourth deduction is arguable but Christianity's Calvinist-Arminian split was not counted either.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Needs careful definition: personal versus impersonal ultimate (−1); the reality of the self (−1); karma with grace and free will (−1). Five.

**Proposed:** keep 5. Strongest reason: the three deductions match the three applied to Christianity and Islam for tensions of the same depth. Confidence 55.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to decide whether a family of mutually refuting schools is one worldview with three unresolved doctrines (5) or several worldviews each scoring 7 or 8, which the site's design does not allow.

## Explanation

**Current:** 7, confidence 55. "Start at 2. Answers all four: a lawful cosmos through cyclic creation, minds through consciousness as fundamental in its monist strand, obligation through dharma, suffering through karma, which gives every case a cause at the price of making every victim responsible: plus 4. Karma's completeness as an account of suffering is treated across the divide as a distinctive resource: plus 1. Seven."

**Evidence:**
- Family-of-systems question. The answers are not confined to one school. Lawful cosmos: Udayana's Nyayakusumanjali (11th century) gives inferential proofs of God from the world as effect and from the ordered combination of atoms, and the cyclic cosmology is inherited by every school (Nyayakusumanjali [21]). Minds: every astika school holds consciousness is not a product of matter: Advaita's Brahman-consciousness, Samkhya's purusha, Nyaya's atman; the file's "in its monist strand" undersells it. Obligation: dharma is the common frame of Mimamsa, Vedanta and the dharmashastra. Suffering: karma is held by every school. So credit for the four answers does not depend on picking a school.
- Distinctive-resource bonus, across the divide. Max Weber, no Hindu, called karma "the most complete formal solution of the problem of theodicy" (Sociology of Religion, via Willett 2015 [14]). Arthur Herman's book-length study calls it the ultimate theodicy and argues it alone leaves no gratuitous suffering (Herman 1976 [13]). Reichenbach, a Christian philosopher, devoted a book to it (Reichenbach 1990 [13]). Whitley Kaufman's attack in Philosophy East and West, the reply by Chadha and Trakakis, Kaufman's rejoinder and Sharma's interjection make a four-round exchange on whether karma works as a systematic theodicy (Kaufman 2005 [11]; Chadha and Trakakis 2007, Kaufman 2007 [12]). A doctrine does not get that treatment unless philosophers across the divide regard it as a distinctive answer.
- A second cross-divide resource exists on minds: Chalmers lists Advaita among the sources of "cosmic idealism" in his taxonomy of mind-body solutions (Chalmers 2019 [15]), and Albahari builds her "perennial idealism" on Advaita's aperspectival consciousness (Albahari 2019 [16]). The bonus is awarded once, so this adds confidence, not points.
- For a lower score: Kaufman's objections (no memory of the deed, no proportionality, infinite regress, free will) are serious. But the rule credits an answer treated as a resource, not one that succeeds; Christianity's suffering God and naturalism's indifferent nature got the bonus on the same basis.

**Rule applied:** Start at 2. Answers: lawful cosmos (+1), minds (+1), obligation (+1), suffering (+1). Distinctive resource across the divide, karma as theodicy (+1). Seven.

**Proposed:** keep 7. Strongest reason: the karma bonus has the best-documented cross-divide standing of any bonus on the list except Christianity's. Confidence 65.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to treat Weber's and Herman's verdicts as admiration for a formal structure rather than a resource for the question, and then apply the same test to the other three bonuses.

## Testability

**Current:** 1, confidence 55. "Exposure 1: Brahman, karma and rebirth are framed so that no experience counts against them. Held, plus 1: contemplative claims about attention and the self, which practice can test and research partly supports. Failed, minus 1: prayer trials, counted once against every tradition that promises answered prayer, which its devotional strands do. Rebirth-memory cases are unconfirmed, which is neutral for every tradition that makes the claim. One."

**Evidence:**
- Meditation: the largest systematic review found moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain, low for stress, and none or insufficient for attention, mood and sleep; Van Dam and 14 co-authors warn the field's claims have outrun its evidence (Goyal 2014 in Van Dam et al. 2018 [23]). "Partly supports" is accurate and the held point matches Buddhism's.
- Prayer: STEP randomised 1,802 bypass patients; complications 52% with prayer versus 51% without, and higher in those who knew they were prayed for (Benson et al. 2006 [22]). The Cochrane review of ten trials, 7,646 patients, found prayer neither beneficial nor harmful (Roberts et al. 2009 [22]). The failed point stands and is applied to every tradition that promises answered prayer.
- Rebirth memories: Stevenson's and Tucker's cases remain unconfirmed; a re-examination of Tucker's strongest American case (Leininger) found it rests almost wholly on parental testimony (Journal of Scientific Exploration [25]; Edwards 1996 [25]). Neutral, as scored.
- A possible second failed kind: Jyotisha, one of the six Vedangas and the basis of horoscope-matching in Hindu marriage, makes checkable predictions, and a double-blind test of 27 astrologers on 200 charts scored marginally below coin-tossing (Narlikar et al. 2009 [24]). Whether astrology is a claim of the worldview or a popular practice is the question; the rule excludes popular claims.

**Rule applied:** Exposure 1. Held: contemplative claims (+1). Failed: prayer trials (−1). Rebirth memories neutral. One.

**Proposed:** keep 1. Strongest reason: every element matches how Buddhism and the theisms were scored. Confidence 55.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to decide whether Jyotisha is a claim of the tradition (then 0) or a popular accretion (then 1); Christianity's failed generation prediction came from its central text, which is the stronger case for a deduction.

## Moral

**Current:** 6, confidence 55. "Start at 8: non-violence, hospitality and devotion extend to outsiders in its best expression. Communal violence and conquest in its name by nationalist movements (minus 1); caste, an exclusion written into its structure and justified from its texts (minus 1); reformers have fought both from within, which the rule does not restore. Six."

**Evidence:**
- Wars and conquest in its name: Human Rights Watch documented state participation in the 2002 Gujarat massacres under a Hindu-nationalist government, with attacks planned and coordinated with officials (HRW 2002 [31]); cow-protection vigilantes killed 44 people, 36 of them Muslim, in 2015-18 with BJP politicians justifying the attacks (HRW 2019 [32]). Premodern conquest "in its name" is weaker than the crusades: Eaton finds Hindu kings looted rival kings' temples as a political act, and Harsha of Kashmir melted temple images for revenue (Eaton 2000 [36]; Rajatarangini VII.1095 [38]). The −1 stands on the modern record, as it does for naturalism.
- Persecution or legal coercion of dissenters and apostates, not counted. Nepal's 1962 constitution declared a Hindu kingdom and barred attempts to convert another person; the 1963 Muluki Ain set three years' imprisonment for attempting conversion and six for completing it, with deportation for foreigners; the 2017 Penal Code keeps up to five years (Thapa, BYU Law Review [26]; ICJ 2018 [27]). The 1854 Muluki Ain ranked the whole population on one caste ladder and set punishments by rank (Khatiwoda, Cubelic, Michaels 2021 [28]). This is a state built on the tradition coercing apostates by statute, the same class that cost Islam a point.
- Indian anti-conversion laws, first in Odisha (1967) and Madhya Pradesh (1968), upheld in Stanislaus (1977), now in about ten states with sentences up to ten years, sponsored by Hindu nationalist governments and applied overwhelmingly against conversion to Christianity and Islam (Stanislaus 1977 [30]; Oxford Human Rights Hub [30]). India is constitutionally secular, so this counts under "the record of the tradition," as nationalist violence already does.
- Legal coercion of a subordinate group: Fukazawa's archival study shows the Peshwa government preserved caste hierarchy "through the legal apparatus of the state," setting penalties by caste (Fukazawa 1968 [29]). The texts prescribe it: Manu 8.267-272 fines a Kshatriya for insulting a Brahmin but has a Shudra's tongue cut out; Gautama Dharmasutra 12.4-6 pours molten tin in a Shudra's ears for hearing the Veda (Olivelle 2005 [37]). This overlaps with the caste deduction below, so the proposal does not rest on it.
- Premodern persecution of religious dissenters is contested: Verardi argues Brahmanical violence drove Buddhism's decline (Verardi 2011 [33]); Lamotte acquits Pushyamitra "through lack of proof," and the Madurai impalement of 8,000 Jains first appears 500 years late and is "little more than an unpleasant legend" for Nilakanta Sastri (Pushyamitra [34]; Madurai [35]). The proposal does not depend on it.
- Exclusion in structure: caste, justified in Manu and the dharmasutras, is uncontested as a textual exclusion (Olivelle 2005 [37]). The −1 stands.
- Best expression: ahimsa and the Gita's equal regard for all beings do extend to outsiders; the start at 8 is right.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Wars and conquest in its name (−1). Persecution or legal coercion of dissenters and apostates: Nepal's conversion ban, Indian anti-conversion laws, Peshwa caste enforcement (−1). Exclusion in its structure: caste (−1). Five.

**Proposed:** change 6→5. Strongest reason: Islam lost a point for "legal coercion of apostates and dissenters under traditional law," and Nepal's Hindu kingdom wrote the same coercion into statute with prison terms, so the same class must cost the same point. Confidence 65.

**If the owner disagrees:** he would have to hold that Nepal's constitution and penal code are not "a state built on" Hinduism, which is harder than the equivalent claim for any state on the list.

## Calibration check

1. **Founding claims against the record.** Judaism: "the patriarchs, Exodus and conquest have little or no archaeological support in the form the texts describe (minus 1)." Islam: "accounts of earlier prophets that conflict with the record where one exists (minus 1)." Hinduism: "Its stories of gods and ages are treated as myth in much of its own tradition and as history in some, which is not a failed claim." Reform Judaism also treats the Exodus as myth; the test was applied to the claim, not the believers' attitude. Reconcile by deducting from Hinduism (proposed) or restoring Judaism and Islam.
2. **Legal coercion of apostates.** Islam: "legal coercion of apostates and dissenters under traditional law (minus 1)." Christianity: "persecution of dissenters, the inquisitions (minus 1)." Hinduism: no entry, though Nepal's Hindu kingdom jailed converters by statute from 1963 and still does. Reconcile by deducting from Hinduism (proposed).
3. **The continuous-community point.** Christianity, Judaism and Hinduism each got +1 for "a continuous, documented community." Islam ("a well-attested founder (plus 1); a text fixed within about a generation (plus 1)") and Buddhism ("a well-attested founder... (plus 1)") did not, though both have one. Reconcile by adding the point to Islam and Buddhism or saying why their communities do not qualify.

## Rule problems (not scored)

- **No founder shields from the late-biography deduction.** Buddhism, Christianity and Islam each lost a history point for late, legendary biographies. Krishna's and Rama's (Harivamsa, Bhagavata Purana, Valmiki) are exactly that, but with "no founder" Hinduism gets neither the +1 nor the −1: the tradition whose central figures are least attested pays nothing for it.
- **Family-of-systems asymmetry.** Under explanation, Hinduism is credited for the best answer any school gives (consciousness-first from Advaita, a designer from Nyaya); under consistency, those schools refuting each other costs one point per doctrine, the price Christianity pays for tensions inside one creed. A family scores better than any member would.
- **Moral rule rewards minority status and thin historiography.** Judaism's file says it: "as a minority faith for most of its history it has little record of persecuting others." Hinduism held state power for two millennia, but its states' religious coercion is less studied and more contested (Verardi versus Lamotte), so it has been scored closer to Judaism than to Christianity. The rule measures record-keeping as much as conduct.
- **Possible double count on caste.** A persecution deduction resting on the Peshwa and Manu evidence alone would charge caste twice (structure and coercion); the proposal rests it on conversion bans instead. The same question applies to Islam's file, where women's legal status and apostasy law are both legal classes.
