# Moral fruit: rescore under the new rule (proposal, 2026-10-28)

Rule as shipped in content/site.json (the report was researched under a longer draft with the same meaning): "Start at 8 for an ethic that, at its best, extends to outsiders. Subtract 1 for each of three classes its authoritative texts or traditional law sanction: war or conquest; coercion of dissenters, apostates or a subordinate group; an exclusion written into its structure, such as caste or slavery. What states did on their own is not scored. Add 1, once, if the tradition reversed one of those classes from within, by its own sources, and the reversal holds in its mainstream. For a stance with no texts, test its canonical statements and count a state that professed it by decree for coercion only."

How the classes were read, so the same fact costs the same point everywhere:

- **Authoritative texts or traditional law** = scripture plus the tradition's own normative law (canon law and the confessions, fiqh, halakha, Vinaya, dharmashastra). Chronicles (Mahavamsa), single theologians (Augustine, Aquinas, Ibn Taymiyya) and state codes (Theodosian Code, Nepal's Muluki Ain, the Sakya code) are not counted unless the tradition's own law adopts them.
- **One deduction per class.** Where a tradition has two facts in a class (slavery and women's legal status in the three Abrahamic faiths and Hinduism; garudhammas and monastic slaves in Buddhism), the second is noted but costs nothing more. This is why women's legal status, present in the texts of five traditions, never moves a score.
- **Reversed from within** = the tradition's own members argued the change from the tradition's own sources, and a member who tried to revive the practice today would be acting against the tradition's mainstream law. The reversal need not declare the old text wrong (Alex's own model case, the rabbinic neutralisation of herem, does not), so the same reading is applied to Islamic abolition. That one reading decides two scores; see Contested calls.
- **A state that professed it by decree** counts for coercion only, as the rule says: the Soviet decrees and 1936 constitution for naturalism, the decree of 18 Floréal Year II for deism.

## Naturalism

**Evidence**
- Canonical statements command none of the three classes. Humanist Manifesto III (2003) grounds ethics in "human need and interest as tested by experience," commits to "treating each person as having inherent worth and dignity," and seeks a world "free of cruelty... where differences are resolved cooperatively without resorting to violence" [1]. Oppy's *Naturalism and Religion* (2018) and Carroll's *The Big Picture* (2016), the two programmatic statements the files already cite, contain no sanction of war, coercion or exclusion [2].
- A state professed it by decree. The RSFSR decree of 23 January (5 February) 1918 separated church from state and school from church [3]; article 13 of the 1918 RSFSR constitution guaranteed "freedom of religious and anti-religious propaganda" and article 65 disenfranchised "monks and clergy of all denominations" [4]; the 1929 Law on Religious Associations stripped religious bodies of legal personality [5]; article 124 of the 1936 USSR constitution recognised "freedom of religious worship and freedom of anti-religious propaganda," a one-sided freedom that, with the 1929 law, is the legal frame for the 1937-38 shooting of clergy [6]. Froese (2008) treats the campaign as an explicitly atheist project [7]. This is coercion of dissenters under a professed stance and counts once.
- Against counting it: Marxism-Leninism is a political doctrine with a materialist premise, not naturalism as its philosophers state it, and Zuckerman's secular Scandinavia has no such record [8]. The new rule answers this: the state "professed it by decree," and the rule charges that for coercion only, not for war or exclusion. The Soviet annexations, charged under the old rule as "wars by states built on it," are no longer scored.
- No exclusion is written into any canonical statement; the 1918 disenfranchisement is the same fact as the coercion already counted.
- Nothing to reverse, so the reform credit cannot apply; naturalism cannot score above 7 under this rule.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Texts sanction none of the three classes. A state that professed it by decree coerced believers and clergy, counted for coercion only (minus 1). No reversal available. Seven.

**Proposed:** 6 → 7. Confidence 65.

**why (ready to paste, 413 chars):**
Start at 8: humanist ethics extends to every person. Its canonical statements, the humanist manifestos and the naturalism of Oppy and Carroll, command no war, no coercion and no exclusion. The one state that professed it by decree, the Soviet Union under its 1918 decree and 1936 constitution, coerced believers and clergy, which counts for coercion only (minus 1). Nothing to reverse, so no reform credit. Seven.

**Sources to add:** "American Humanist Association, Humanism and Its Aspirations: Humanist Manifesto III (2003)"; "Paul Froese, The Plot to Kill God: Findings from the Soviet Experiment in Secularization (2008)".

## Buddhism

**Evidence**
- War: the Pali canon and every Vinaya contain no command or sanction of war; Harvey's standard survey (2000, ch. "War and Peace") finds the early texts give no justification for war and treats the later exceptions as marginal [9]. The Mahavamsa's "one and a half human beings" passage (ch. 25) is a chronicle, not law, and the Buddhism report already treats it so [10].
- War, the other way: two Mahayana scriptures sanction armed force. The *Satyakaparivarta* (Arya-Bodhisattva-gocara-upayavisaya-vikurvana-nirdesa) tells a king he may wage defensive war with compassionate intent and escape the karma of killing; Jenkins (2010, 2011) reads it as "religious sanction for engaging in warfare," Sugiki (2020) as an ethic for kings that limits but permits war [11][12]. The Mahayana *Mahaparinirvana Sutra* tells laymen to protect the Dharma "with swords and staves" but "never use them to take life" [13]. Both are canonical in East Asia and Tibet; neither is the tradition's normative law, and both hedge the sanction more than any Abrahamic or Hindu text does. Scored as contested, no deduction; see Contested calls.
- Coercion: the Vinaya's only sanctions are against members (expulsion for parajika, probation, the "overturned bowl" boycott of a layman); no text or monastic code commands coercion of non-members, apostates or dissenters. Myanmar's 2015 religion laws, the 1928 Thai Sangha order and Tibet's penal code are state acts and are no longer scored.
- Exclusion: the eight garudhammas (AN 8.51; Cullavagga X) make a nun of a hundred years bow to a monk ordained that day, bar nuns from admonishing monks, and make nuns' ordination depend on monks; they stand in every surviving Vinaya [14]. Anālayo finds their historicity doubtful but confirms they are canonical in all schools [15]. The Vinaya also bars slaves from ordination and lets monasteries accept bonded labourers; Silk documents institutional monastic ownership of slaves across Asia [16]. Same class, one deduction.
- Reform: the bhikkhuni revival (Sarnath 1996, Bodh Gaya 1998) argues from the Vinaya's own single-sangha ordination provision; Bhutan's Je Khenpo ordained 144 nuns in June 2022 and 265 in November 2025 [17]; Sri Lanka's Supreme Court on 16 June 2025 upheld a bhikkhuni's right to an identity card and found the chief prelates had no authority to stop ordinations [18]. But the Sri Lankan chief prelates told the court the revival is invalid under Vinaya, Thailand's 1928 Sangha order still bars it, Myanmar jails nuns who ordain abroad [19], and the garudhammas themselves are unchanged even where bhikkhunis exist (East Asia never lost them and keeps the rules). The reversal does not yet hold in the mainstream; no credit.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Texts sanction no war that the tradition's law adopts, and no coercion. The garudhammas are an exclusion written into its structure (minus 1). The bhikkhuni revival is argued from within but is rejected by the Thai, Burmese and Sri Lankan hierarchies, so no credit. Seven.

**Proposed:** 5 → 7. Confidence 60.

**why (ready to paste, 541 chars):**
Start at 8: compassion for all sentient beings extends to every creature. Its canon and Vinaya command no war and no coercion of dissenters; the Mahayana texts that let a king fight in defence are minor and hedged. The eight garudhammas, which subordinate every nun to every monk in every surviving Vinaya, are an exclusion written into its structure (minus 1). The bhikkhuni revival argues from the Vinaya but is rejected by the Thai, Burmese and Sri Lankan hierarchies, so it does not yet hold in the mainstream and earns no credit. Seven.

**Sources to add:** "Peter Harvey, An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics (2000)"; "Stephen Jenkins, Making Merit through Warfare, in Buddhist Warfare, ed. Jerryson and Juergensmeyer (2010)".

## Christianity

**Evidence**
- War: the canon it inherits commands conquest (Deuteronomy 20:16-18, Joshua) [20]; its own law adopted holy war: Fourth Lateran Council canon 3 (1215) grants those "who have girded themselves with the cross for the extermination of the heretics" the same indulgence as crusaders to the Holy Land, and canon 71 organises the crusade itself [21]. Just-war teaching from Augustine to the Catechism (2309) sanctions war under conditions. Minus 1 on the tradition's own law, not only on inherited texts.
- Coercion: Lateran IV canon 3 hands condemned heretics "to the secular rulers" and makes rulers swear "to exterminate" heretics from their lands [21]; Innocent IV's *Ad extirpanda* (1252) authorises torture of heretics and obliges civil authorities under pain of excommunication [22]; the Westminster Confession 23.3 (1646) charges the magistrate to see "that all blasphemies and heresies be suppressed" [23]. Kamen's lower inquisition numbers do not remove the sanction from the law [24].
- Exclusion: the New Testament regulates slavery without condemning it (Ephesians 6:5, 1 Timothy 6:1, Philemon); the Council of Gangra (c. 340, canon 3) anathematises anyone who teaches a slave to despise or leave his master; *Dum Diversas* (1452) licensed perpetual enslavement of "Saracens and pagans." Noll shows the proslavery argument was the plain reading of the texts and antislavery readers had to appeal to the gospel's spirit [25]. Minus 1.
- Reform, slavery: abolition was argued from the same scriptures by its own members, the Germantown Quakers (1688), Clarkson and Wilberforce, from the Golden Rule, Galatians 3:28 and Exodus [25]; Gregory XVI's *In Supremo Apostolatus* (1839) condemned the trade "of Negroes and all other men" [26]; the Catechism (2414) now forbids whatever leads "to the enslavement of human beings." No mainstream church defends slavery. Credit, once.
- Reform, coercion (noted, not double-credited): *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965) declares all persons "immune from coercion" in religion, grounds it in "the word of God," and admits past conduct "opposed to" the gospel [27]; American Presbyterians struck the suppression clause from Westminster 23.3 in 1788 [23]. Rhonheimer reads the Catholic change as a corrective break, Pink as continuity; either way it holds [28].
- Best expression stands: love of enemies and the dignity of every person extend to outsiders; start at 8.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Texts and canon law sanction war (minus 1), coercion of heretics (minus 1), slavery (minus 1). Reversed from within: abolition argued from scripture, taught by Rome, held everywhere (plus 1). Six.

**Proposed:** 5 → 6. Confidence 80.

**why (ready to paste, 507 chars):**
Start at 8: love of enemies and the dignity of every person extend to outsiders. Its texts and canon law sanction war, from the conquest commands it inherits to the crusade indulgence of Lateran IV (minus 1); coercion of heretics, handed to the secular arm by the same council and by Ad extirpanda (minus 1); and slavery, regulated in both testaments and canon law (minus 1). Abolition was argued from the same scriptures by Quakers and evangelicals, then taught by Rome, and holds everywhere (plus 1). Six.

**Sources to add:** "Fourth Lateran Council, canon 3, On Heretics (1215)"; "Mark Noll, The Civil War as a Theological Crisis (2006)".

## Deism

**Evidence**
- Canonical statements command none of the three classes. Herbert's five common notions (1624) are offered as the religion of all men; Tindal (1730) makes the law of nature binding on everyone alike; Paine (1794) makes our duties "to do justice, love mercy, and endeavour to make our fellow-creatures happy" [29]. None sanctions war, coercion or a subordinate class; Jefferson's slaveholding is a personal fact and the 1775 anti-slavery essay's attribution to Paine is disputed [30].
- A state professed it by decree. The Convention's decree of 18 Floréal Year II (7 May 1794) begins "The French people recognise the existence of the Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul" and defines its worship as "the practice of the duties of man" [31]. Robespierre presided at the Festival of 20 Prairial (8 June) [32].
- Coercion under that profession: the Law of 22 Prairial (10 June 1794), two days after the Festival, removed defence counsel and witnesses and left juries only acquittal or death; about 1,400 people were guillotined in Paris in the seven weeks to Thermidor [33]. Smyth (2016) treats the cult as Robespierre's search for a republican morality rather than a cynical tool [32]. Counted for coercion only, as the rule says.
- Against counting it: the Terror began in September 1793, before the decree, and its victims were political enemies rather than religious dissenters; the decree itself coerced no one. The rule's test is whether a state that professed the stance coerced dissenters, and the Great Terror is that. The same looseness is applied to naturalism's Soviet case.
- Theophilanthropy (1796-1801), the other deist community with state backing, has no record of coercion [34]. Nothing to reverse, so no credit; deism cannot score above 7.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Texts sanction none of the three classes. A state that professed it by decree ran the Great Terror, counted for coercion only (minus 1). No reversal available. Seven.

**Proposed:** 5 → 7. Confidence 55.

**why (ready to paste, 430 chars):**
Start at 8: an ethic of reason, benevolence and tolerance, from Herbert to Paine, extends to everyone. Its canonical statements command no war, no coercion and no exclusion. The one state that professed it by decree, the French republic under the decree of 18 Floréal 1794, ran the Great Terror under the Law of 22 Prairial six weeks later, which counts for coercion only (minus 1). Nothing to reverse, so no reform credit. Seven.

**Sources to add:** "Jonathan Smyth, Robespierre and the Festival of the Supreme Being (2016)"; "National Convention, Decree of 18 Floréal Year II (1794)".

## Islam

**Evidence**
- War: Quran 9:5 and 9:29 command fighting the polytheists and the People of the Book "until they pay the jizya" [35]; classical fiqh in all schools treats offensive jihad to extend the abode of Islam as a collective duty (fard kifaya) declarable by the ruler, and Peters (1996) shows the defensive-only reading is the modern development [36]. Minus 1.
- Coercion: Peters and De Vries (1976) document that all four Sunni schools and the Shia prescribe death for the male apostate, with repentance offered first in most [37]; dhimmi status under Quran 9:29 and the Pact of Umar made non-Muslims protected but "second-class subjects" (Cohen) [38]. Against: al-Alwani, Kamali and others argue the Quran itself sets no temporal penalty, and Egypt's mufti said so in 2007, but apostasy law remains in force in several states and in the classical manuals. Minus 1.
- Exclusion: Quran 4:3, 23:5-6 and 24:33 permit slavery and concubinage; Brown (2019), a believing scholar, writes that their permissibility "is undeniable in the Qur'an" and that no classical scholar called for prohibition; Kecia Ali shows marriage law was built on the ownership idiom [39][40]. Minus 1.
- Reform, for: Ahmad Bey abolished slavery in Tunisia in 1846 on a justification of Islamic justice (al-'adl) and freedom (hurriya), though Montana locates the initiative at court rather than among the ulema [41]; Brown records that by 1900 many leading Muslim scholars agreed slavery should be prohibited [39]; the 2014 Open Letter to al-Baghdadi, signed by 126 scholars including the muftis of Egypt and Jordan, states that "the re-introduction of slavery is forbidden in Islam. It was abolished by universal consensus," arguing from Quran 90:12-14 and 58:3 [42]. Every Muslim-majority state bans it. A Muslim who enslaved captives today would act against the mainstream's law.
- Reform, against: a 2016 review in the *Journal of International Humanitarian Action* accepts the letter's substance but doubts there was ever a scholarly ijma, locating abolition in state law and the Slavery Conventions [43]; Brown concludes Muslims cannot call slavery intrinsically immoral without impugning the Quran, so the mainstream holds it prohibited by the ruler's order and consensus, not wrong in itself [39]; Mauritania abolished only in 1981, criminalised in 2007 and 2015, and the practice persists [44]; Islamic State revived it on textual grounds in 2014. See Contested calls: the credit stands or falls with Judaism's.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Texts and classical law sanction war (minus 1), coercion of apostates and dhimmis (minus 1), slavery (minus 1). Reversed from within: abolition argued from the Quran by its scholars, held by consensus and in every state's law (plus 1). Six.

**Proposed:** 5 → 6. Confidence 50.

**why (ready to paste, 529 chars):**
Start at 8: obligatory charity, equality before God and hospitality extend to outsiders. Its texts and classical law sanction war, the sword and jizya verses and offensive jihad as a collective duty (minus 1); coercion, the death penalty for apostasy in all four Sunni schools and dhimmi status (minus 1); and slavery, permitted in the Quran and regulated in fiqh (minus 1). Abolition was argued from the Quran's emancipation verses by its scholars and now holds by consensus, as the 2014 letter to Baghdadi states (plus 1). Six.

**Sources to add:** "Jonathan A. C. Brown, Slavery and Islam (2019)"; "Open Letter to Dr Ibrahim Awwad al-Badri, alias Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi (2014)"; "Rudolph Peters, Jihad in Classical and Modern Islam (1996)".

## Judaism

**Evidence**
- War: Deuteronomy 20:16-18 commands that of the seven nations "you shall not let a soul remain alive," and Deuteronomy 25:19 the blotting out of Amalek [20]; Maimonides codifies both as obligatory war (milchemet mitzvah) and rules that "anyone who chances upon one of them and does not kill him violates a negative commandment" (Kings and Wars 5:1, 5:4) [45]. Minus 1 on the texts and the law, not on the conquest's historicity, which the history line already finds unsupported.
- Coercion: Deuteronomy 13:7-12 orders the death of the one who entices to other gods, 13:13-19 the destruction of the idolatrous city, 17:2-7 stoning for idolatry [20]; Maimonides rules "it is a mitzvah to kill minim and apikorsim" and, where one cannot act openly, to remove the ladder from the cistern (Murderer 4:10) [46]. Minus 1.
- Exclusion: Leviticus 25:44-46 makes foreign slaves "property... for all time" [20]; the Shulchan Aruch (Yoreh Deah 267) still codifies the Canaanite slave; Hezser shows Jewish slaveholding through antiquity [47]. Women's testimony and divorce rules (Yevamot 14:1) are the same class and cost nothing more. Minus 1.
- Reform, war: Mishnah Yadayim 4:4 rules that "Sennacherib came and mixed up all the nations," so the biblical peoples cannot be identified [48]; Maimonides rules the seven nations' "memory has already been obliterated" (5:4) and requires that peace be offered before any war, "whether optional or obligatory," with the ban falling only on those who refuse (6:1, 6:4) [45]; Golinkin's responsum shows this has been the halakhic position for two millennia [49]. The same move neutralised capital law: Makkot 1:10 calls a court that executes once in seventy years destructive, and Avodah Zarah 8b records capital jurisdiction ceasing forty years before the Temple fell [50]. A Jew who tried to carry out herem today would act against mainstream halakha in every denomination. Credit, once.
- Against the credit: Orthodox halakha holds the commands valid in principle and inoperative only because the nations are lost and the Sanhedrin is gone, so the reversal is a suspension, not a repudiation; religious-nationalist invocation of Amalek since 2023 shows the text is still live rhetorically, though no rabbinic court has applied it. This is the same shape as Islamic abolition and is credited on the same reading.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Texts and halakha command war (minus 1), coercion of the idolater and the heretic (minus 1), permanent slavery of foreigners (minus 1). Reversed from within: herem neutralised by the Mishnah and Maimonides, held across the mainstream (plus 1). Six.

**Proposed:** 5 → 6. Confidence 60.

**why (ready to paste, 546 chars):**
Start at 8: the prophetic ethic of justice for the poor and the stranger extends to outsiders. Its texts and halakha command war, the herem of Deuteronomy 20 codified as obligatory war (minus 1); coercion, death for the enticer and the idolater in Deuteronomy 13 and 17 and Maimonides on heretics (minus 1); and permanent slavery of foreigners in Leviticus 25 and the Shulchan Aruch (minus 1). The rabbis neutralised herem from within, ruling the seven nations lost and peace terms owed to all, and that holds across the mainstream (plus 1). Six.

**Sources to add:** "Moses Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings and Wars 5-6"; "Catherine Hezser, Jewish Slavery in Antiquity (2005)".

## Hinduism

**Evidence**
- War: the Gita makes fighting the kshatriya's duty, "a righteous war... an open door to heaven" (2:31-32, 2:37) [51]; Manu 7.89 sends kings who fall "fighting with utmost power" to heaven, and 7.99-101 tell the king to "strive to gain what he has not yet gained" [52]. Dharmashastra is the tradition's own law. Minus 1. Eaton's finding that temple raids were political, not religious, no longer matters: states are not scored.
- Coercion: Manu 9.225 orders the king to banish "members of heretical sects" from his town [53]; Manu 8.270-272 cut out the tongue of the once-born who insults a twice-born, drive an iron nail into his mouth, pour hot oil in his ears; Gautama Dharmasutra 12.4-6 pours molten tin into the ears of a Shudra who hears the Veda (Olivelle 2005) [54]. Minus 1 on heretics and the Shudra penalties; Nepal's conversion ban and India's anti-conversion statutes are state acts and no longer scored.
- Exclusion: Manu 8.413-414, "one should make a Shudra perform servitude, whether he is bought or not bought, for he was created solely for the servitude of a brahmana," and "a Shudra, though emancipated by his master, is not released from servitude" [55]; Manu 1.31 and 10 fix the four varnas and the outcast groups. Minus 1.
- Reform, for: Gandhi argued against untouchability from the Gita and Upanishads; the 1969 Udupi conference of dharmacharyas passed unanimously that untouchability has no place in Hindu religious or social affairs, "Hindavah sodarah sarve, na Hindu patito bhavet" [56]; the Vishva Hindu Parishad reports training 5,000 Dalit priests; article 17 of the 1950 Constitution abolished untouchability (a state act, not credited).
- Reform, against: the class scored is caste, not untouchability; no Hindu law text has been revised and varna is still taught as divinely ordained (Gita 4:13); the Puri Shankaracharya defended the caste system in 2023 against the RSS chief's own criticism [57]; Thorat and Joshi (EPW 2020) find 27 percent of Indian households, 30 percent of Hindu households, admit practising untouchability (IHDS 2011-12) [58]. The reversal is partial in doctrine and does not hold in practice. No credit.

**Rule applied:** Start at 8. Dharmashastra and the Gita sanction war (minus 1), banishment of heretics and corporal penalties on Shudras (minus 1), caste (minus 1). Untouchability is disavowed by many acharyas but caste itself stands in the law and in some of its heads' teaching, so no reversal holds. Five.

**Proposed:** keep 5. Confidence 55.

**why (ready to paste, 534 chars):**
Start at 8: non-violence, hospitality and equal regard for all beings extend to outsiders in its best expression. Its own law sanctions war, the Gita's duty of the kshatriya and Manu's heaven for kings who fall in battle (minus 1); coercion, Manu's banishment of heretics and corporal penalties on Shudras (minus 1); and caste, with the Shudra created to serve (minus 1). Untouchability has been disavowed by many acharyas, but caste itself stands in its law and in the pronouncements of some of its heads, so no reversal holds. Five.

**Sources to add:** "Patrick Olivelle, Manu's Code of Law (2005)"; "Amit Thorat and Omkar Joshi, The Continuing Practice of Untouchability in India, Economic and Political Weekly 55 (2020)".

## Contested calls

1. **Islam, reform credit (6 or 5).** Credited because the mainstream's own scholars argue abolition from the Quran and hold reintroduction forbidden (2014 letter, 126 signatories) [42]. The one fact Alex would weigh: Brown's conclusion that no classical scholar prohibited slavery and that the modern mainstream treats it as suspended by the ruler's decree and consensus, not wrong in itself [39]. If that disqualifies the credit, the same logic disqualifies Judaism's (next item), and both go to 5.
2. **Judaism, reform credit (6 or 5).** Credited because Mishnah and Maimonides, from the tradition's own sources, made herem inoperative and the whole mainstream holds it so [45][48]. The one fact to weigh: Orthodox halakha keeps the command valid in principle (Kings and Wars 5:4 still says omitting to kill one of the seven nations breaks a negative commandment); the neutralisation is "they can no longer be identified," a suspension. Alex's own MORAL-RULE.md names this case as the model reversal, so the report credits it, and credits Islam on the same reading.
3. **Buddhism, war (7 or 6).** Scored no because the Vinaya and early canon contain nothing and the tradition's best survey (Harvey) treats the Mahayana exceptions as marginal [9]. The one fact to weigh: the *Satyakaparivarta*, a canonical sutra, tells a king he may wage defensive war with compassionate intent and avoid the karma of killing (Jenkins 2010; Sugiki 2020) [11][12]. If a canonical sanction of defensive war counts the way Lateran IV's indulgence counts, Buddhism is 6.
4. **Buddhism, reform credit (7 or 8).** Not credited because the Thai, Burmese and Sri Lankan hierarchies reject bhikkhuni ordination and the garudhammas themselves stand unchanged everywhere [18][19]. The one fact to weigh: Bhutan's Je Khenpo, head of a national sangha, ordained 144 nuns in 2022 and 265 in 2025, and Sri Lanka's Supreme Court recognised the order in June 2025 [17][18]. If "mainstream" means a growing plurality rather than the hierarchies, Buddhism is 8.
5. **Hinduism, reform credit (5 or 6).** Not credited because caste, the class scored, is not reversed; only untouchability is disavowed, and 30 percent of Hindu households still practise it [58]. The one fact to weigh: the 1969 Udupi declaration, passed unanimously by the assembled acharyas from Hindu sources, is the same kind of act as *In Supremo* or the Baghdadi letter [56]. If untouchability's disavowal counts as reversing the exclusion, Hinduism is 6.
6. **Deism, coercion (7 or 8).** Charged because the state that professed deism by decree ran the Great Terror under that profession. The one fact to weigh: the Terror began eight months before the decree and its victims were political, not religious, dissenters [33]. If the decree must have caused the coercion, deism is 8, and naturalism's Soviet charge needs the same test (the 1929 law and 1937-38 shootings would still pass it).
7. **Naturalism, coercion (7 or 8).** Charged because the rule's own note names the Soviet decrees. The one fact to weigh: the 1918 decree itself grants every citizen the right "to adopt any religion or not to adopt any"; the coercion is in article 65, the 1929 law and the terror, which are state acts under a professed stance [3][4][5]. The rule counts a professed stance's coercion, so 7 stands.
8. **Christianity, war on its own law (not just inherited text).** Some would charge Christianity only for inherited Deuteronomy, which it reads as superseded. Lateran IV canon 3 and 71 close that door: the church's own council granted indulgences for armed extermination of heretics and organised the crusade [21]. No alternative score.
